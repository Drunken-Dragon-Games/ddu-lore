# Writing Systems (Neography) and Romanization for a Norse-Flavored Fantasy Culture

Research notes compiled 2026-11-19. Use case: a Norse-flavored region whose old runes are both writing and dangerous magic. Rune *magic* is forbidden in a star-worshipping city-state (Auristar), which may therefore have developed a second, non-runic "star" script. The franchise look is high fashion and techwear over Viking shapes and star motifs. The script must work in art, architecture (carved white stone, stained-glass domes) and game UI.

## 1. Script typologies (alphabet, abjad, abugida, syllabary, logography, featural): trade-offs and how conlangers choose

### Takeaway
The main choice is how many sounds (or how much meaning) one glyph carries. That choice should follow the language's syllable structure and morphology: syllabaries and logographies suit simple syllables, abjads suit consonant-root morphology, and alphabets are the most flexible and the easiest to make fonts for. Featural design, where a glyph's shape encodes sound features as in Hangul and Tengwar, is a styling layer you can put on any type, and it is the strongest way to make a script feel engineered rather than ciphered.

### Cited Findings
**Definitions (academic course material)**
- UCL's PLIN0068 *Constructed Languages* lecture gives these definitions. Logographic: each glyph stands for a concept (morpheme, word or phrase). Syllabary: one symbol per syllable (Japanese kana, which are strictly moras; Cherokee; Afaka). Abjad: one symbol per consonant (Phoenician, Arabic, Hebrew). Abugida: a consonant glyph with an inherent vowel, usually [a], and other vowels shown by diacritics (Brahmi and its descendants, Ge'ez). Alphabet: separate consonant and vowel symbols (Greek, Uyghur, Hangul). — [UCL PLIN0068 Lecture 4](https://www.homepages.ucl.ac.uk/~ucjtudo/assets/teaching/PLIN0068/4_writing_systems.html)
- On fit: "Syllabaries work for languages with simple syllable structures," and "Abjads make sense for semitic languages, which use templa[t]ic morphology with consonants as roots." — [UCL PLIN0068](https://www.homepages.ucl.ac.uk/~ucjtudo/assets/teaching/PLIN0068/4_writing_systems.html)
- The same lecture's design exercise has students decide (a) the type of system, (b) the direction (L→R, R→L, top→bottom or bottom→top) and (c) the form, meaning the material and main writing instrument. — [UCL PLIN0068](https://www.homepages.ucl.ac.uk/~ucjtudo/assets/teaching/PLIN0068/4_writing_systems.html)

**Trade-offs (conlang guides)**
- Logographies need thousands of glyphs and are hard to build. They suit languages with many short, invariant words, and components can combine into semantic and phonetic hints (the rebus principle). Syllabaries work best with simple syllables, and diacritics such as voicing marks can cut the glyph count. Abjads are economical but ambiguous if the grammar depends on vowel changes. Alphabets are flexible and familiar, but "a plain one-to-one match with your phonology can feel dull." Abugidas feel distinctive but are harder to read at first and harder to make fonts for. Featural scripts (e.g., Hangul) can sit on any of these structures. — [Conlang Guide: Writing Systems](https://conlang-guide.bearblog.dev/deep-dives-writing-systems/)
- "an alphabet is still the easiest type of script to make a font for"; abugidas and syllabaries need many ligatures, and logographies are very hard to make fonts for. — [Conlang Guide](https://conlang-guide.bearblog.dev/deep-dives-writing-systems/)
- "a writing system is not a language". You can change the script without changing the language, a language can have several scripts, and writing direction is independent of script type. — [Conlang Guide](https://conlang-guide.bearblog.dev/deep-dives-writing-systems/)
- Rosenfelder: "Logographic scripts and syllabaries tend to work best with languages that have a very limited syllabic structure." Japanese suits them and English is a poor fit. He points to Chinese, the Japanese and Maya syllabaries, Sanskrit, Korean's "featural code" and all-cursive Arabic as models to study. — [Zompist, Language Construction Kit](https://www.zompist.com/kitlong.html)
- Wikibooks classifies scripts by how many sounds each glyph carries (syllabary, consonant-only, consonants with vowel marks, separate consonants and vowels, logographic), notes that the types can be combined, and advises against copying the Roman script. — [Wikibooks: Conlang/Beginner/Writing](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Conlang/Beginner/Writing)
- Neography.info's step 1 asks: "Do characters represent the sounds of speech, or the meanings of words, or a mix of both?" Then list every character and its value, and decide the writing direction *before* designing glyphs. — [Neography.info: Create a script](https://neography.info/create-a-script/)
- The Conlang Guide recommends finishing the phonology before designing the script. — [Conlang Guide](https://conlang-guide.bearblog.dev/deep-dives-writing-systems/)

**Featural systems**
- In a featural writing system, "the shapes of the symbols (such as letters) are not arbitrary"; symbols "encode phonological features of the phonemes that they represent." The term comes from Geoffrey Sampson. Examples are Hangul, Tengwar, Pitman shorthand and Visible Speech, with Canadian Aboriginal syllabics partly featural. Hangul's featural status is disputed: John DeFrancis rejects the label. — [Wikipedia: Featural writing system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Featural_writing_system)
- In Hangul, featural symbols "are combined into alphabetic letters," which are then combined into syllable blocks, giving three levels of representation. — [Wikipedia: Featural writing system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Featural_writing_system)
- Tengwar: "the shapes of the letters correspond to the distinctive features of the sounds they represent". There are four series (*témar*) by place of articulation and six grades (*tyeller*) by manner. Doubling the bow voices a stop, raising the stem makes a fricative, and shortening it makes a nasal. — [Wikipedia: Tengwar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tengwar)

**Purpose drives choice**
- A conlang slide deck surfaced in search suggests first asking what the script is *for* (journaling, games, a secret code, art, a conlang, scholarship). A code shared among friends should stay simple. — [Google Slides deck on constructed writing (search snippet)](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1WIXH1t5D06G1XUFjnISNrlGPkCqzlASP/htmlpresent)

### Inferences
- Old Norse-style phonology has heavy consonant clusters and many vowel qualities (æ, ø, ǫ, plus length; see Q5). Clusters rule out a pure syllabary or logography, and an abjad discards the vowel distinctions Norse-flavored names depend on. For the **runic** script, a historically grounded **alphabet** with an intentionally *under-specified* inventory (Younger-Futhark-style, where one rune covers several sounds) fits both the history and the "arcane, ambiguous" feel.
- For the **star script**, a **featural alphabet or abugida** fits a scholarly, astronomy-obsessed city-state well. Shapes can encode sound classes through star geometry (number of rays or points = place of articulation, a ring or halo = voicing, and so on), on the model of Tengwar's témar/tyeller grid. That contrasts sharply with the unsystematic, "found" feel of runes, and makes Auristar's script read as rational and engineered in-world.
- Because fonts are easiest for alphabets and abugidas need many ligatures, a star *abugida* raises UI engineering cost (see Q6). An alphabet with optional ornamental ligatures is the cheapest route to good in-game text.

### Gaps
- Omniglot's neography pages and the r/neography wiki definitions could not be retrieved (403 and blocked), so their versions of the typology are not quoted.
- I found no source describing Hangul's articulatory shape logic (the tongue and mouth shapes in the Hunminjeongeum Haerye). The Wikipedia featural page did not cover it.

## 2. How medium and culture shape glyphs; runic history, bind runes, rune names, and Norse attitudes to rune magic

### Takeaway
The tool and the surface leave a strong mark on letterforms. Clay and a reed stylus produced wedges, carving produced straight strokes with few or no horizontals, Ogham's stone edge produced strokes off a stemline, and palm leaf and brush produced curves. Runes are the textbook case of angular, carving-adapted script, but the "avoid horizontals because of wood grain" theory is a widely held *inference*, not a proven fact. Runic history offers ready-made worldbuilding beats: reduction (24→16), expansion (Futhorc to 33), carving vs. everyday variants, displacement by a church-backed script, and a long afterlife as "magic." Primary sources contain genuine rune-magic motifs (Hávamál, Sigrdrífumál, Egil's Saga, curse stones), but museum scholarship stresses that most inscriptions are mundane.

### Cited Findings
**Medium → shape (general)**
- Cuneiform takes its name from its "wedge-shaped impressions" (Latin *cuneus*). Early signs were *drawn* in clay with a pointed reed. In the mid-3rd millennium BC a wedge-tipped stylus *pressed* into the clay "made writing quicker and easier, especially when writing on soft clay." Signs were rotated 90° counterclockwise (reason unknown). The inventory fell from about 1,500 signs to about 600, Assyrian forms became "significantly more abstract," and all signs reduced to five basic wedges: horizontal, vertical, two diagonals, and the *Winkelhaken*. A "linear" pointed-stylus style persisted on stone monuments until about 2000 BC. — [Wikipedia: Cuneiform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuneiform)
- The cuneiform *method* was reused for very different systems: Old Persian (a semi-alphabet under Darius, 5th c. BC) and Ugaritic, "a standard Semitic style alphabet (an abjad) written using the cuneiform method." — [Wikipedia: Cuneiform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuneiform)
- "Writing on palm leaves is thought to have given rise to the curly forms of Brahmic scripts." Chinese, Korean and Japanese shapes come from brush on bamboo or paper. — [UCL PLIN0068](https://www.homepages.ucl.ac.uk/~ucjtudo/assets/teaching/PLIN0068/4_writing_systems.html)
- "Carved or etched letters will tend to have hard lines"; brush-written letters tend to flow; scripts derived from knotted strings may have a connecting line through each glyph. — [Wikibooks: Conlang/Beginner/Writing](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Conlang/Beginner/Writing)
- Runes are angular because "carving tends to be easier when you're making straight lines and sharp angles." — [Conlang Guide](https://conlang-guide.bearblog.dev/deep-dives-writing-systems/)
- Tool-specific stroke logic: with a brush, strokes thicken under pressure and thin when dragged ("follow the general rightward and downward stroke direction as much as possible"). A fountain or marker pen gives thin horizontals and thick downstrokes, and a wide-tip pen varies width with stroke direction. "cursive is practical, not decorative" (it is driven by speed and fewer pen lifts). — [Neography.info: Create a script](https://neography.info/create-a-script/)
- **Ogham**: orthodox inscriptions (4th–6th c. AD, about 400 surviving, about 60% of the Irish ones in Cork and Kerry) were cut along the *edge* of a standing stone, which "formed the stemline against which individual characters are cut". They are read from the bottom left upward along the edge. There were 20 letters in four groups by stroke angle and direction, plus 5 later *forfeda*. Phonological evidence suggests an earlier period of writing on wood or other perishables. Theories of origin include a cipher "designed by the Irish to hide their meaning from writers of the Latin alphabet" or an invention of early Christian communities. — [Wikipedia: Ogham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ogham)
- Ogham is "sometimes even written in a circle"; line direction (e.g., boustrophedon) is a separate choice from text direction. — [Wikibooks](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Conlang/Beginner/Writing)

**Runes and the wood-grain theory (with caveats)**
- Wikipedia's *Runes* article: rune branches "tend not to run horizontally – a tendency which is often assumed to indicate that runes were originally designed to be cut in wood". Horizontal and curved lines *along* the grain are avoided "as they would easily disappear." The article also notes the angular shapes "are shared with most contemporary alphabets of the period that were used for carving in wood or stone." — [Wikipedia: Runes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runes) (retrieved via search and the [EncycloReader mirror](https://encycloreader.org/r/wikipedia.php?q=Runes))
- Counter-evidence: the Elder Futhark article says the angular shapes likely suit incision in wood or metal, but this is shared with Old Italic alphabets. Early inscriptions *do* show horizontal strokes (in e, t, l, ŋ and h), while "mature" 6th–8th-century runes tend to use three stroke directions. Runic *manuscripts* (e.g., Codex Runicus) also have horizontals. — [Wikipedia: Elder Futhark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elder_Futhark); [Wikipedia: Runes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runes)
- The wood-grain view also implies that early runic writing before AD 500 favored perishable wood that is now almost entirely lost. This appeared in the same search set; a [2015 Journal of Historical Sociolinguistics article](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/jhsl-2015-0004/html) surfaced but was not fetched to confirm attribution.

**Elder Futhark (origin, structure, names)**
- 24 runes in three *ættir* of eight. Inscriptions date from the 2nd to the 8th century. Direction is left-to-right, with boustrophedon attested. The oldest inscription is the Vimose Comb (c. 160 AD). The Kylver Stone (c. 400) has "the earliest known sequential listing of the alphabet." The longest inscription, Eggjum (early 8th c.), has "some 200 characters." About 350 inscriptions are known. The script was deciphered by Sophus Bugge in 1865. — [Wikipedia: Elder Futhark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elder_Futhark)
- Origin: "commonly believed to originate in the Old Italic scripts" (Etruscan or Rhaetic, or Latin itself). f, a, i, t, m and l are generally accepted as matching Latin letters, and about ten runes have disputed sources. The script was "clearly designed for epigraphic purposes," and one scholarly view calls its earliest form an "artificial, playful, not really needed imitation of the Roman script." — [Wikipedia: Elder Futhark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elder_Futhark)
- Reconstructed names come from daily life and myth: *fehu* (cattle, wealth), *ansuz* (god), *raidō* (ride, journey), *berkanan* (birch), *ehwaz* (horse), *mannaz* (man), *dagaz* (day). — [Wikipedia: Elder Futhark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elder_Futhark)

**Elder → Younger Futhark (reduction)**
- "By the late 8th century, the reduction from 24 to 16 runes was complete," after a 7th–8th-century transition. Paradoxically, this coincided with sound changes that *increased* the number of phonemes. — [Wikipedia: Younger Futhark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Younger_Futhark)
- Result: one rune, many sounds. The u-rune covers u, v, w, y, o and ø; the k-rune covers k, g and ŋ; þ covers þ and ð; i covers i and e. "the difference between voiced and unvoiced consonants was no longer expressed in writing." A convention against carving the same rune twice in a row erased vowel length, so distinct words were spelled identically. — [Wikipedia: Younger Futhark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Younger_Futhark)
- Variants by function: **long-branch** (Danish, suggested for "documentation on stone"), **short-twig/Rök** (Swedish and Norwegian, suggested for everyday messages on wood; 9 simplified forms and 7 identical), and **staveless/Hälsinge** runes (10th–12th c.), which "lack certain strokes" and have no Unicode range. The functional explanation is only "general opinion" and is contested. — [Wikipedia: Younger Futhark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Younger_Futhark)
- **Medieval runes** re-expanded to "one sign for each phoneme": "Dotted variants of voiceless signs were introduced to denote the corresponding voiced consonants." They were used until the 15th century. More than 600 Bergen inscriptions, mostly on wooden sticks, have been found since the 1950s. About 3,000 Younger Futhark runestones survive. — [Wikipedia: Younger Futhark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Younger_Futhark)
- The 16 Younger Futhark names, with meanings from the rune poems: *fé* (wealth), *úr* (sleet or drizzle), *þurs* (giant), *óss* (god/Æsir), *reið* (riding), *kaun* (ulcer), *hagall* (hail), *nauðr* (need), *íss* (ice), *ár* (year, harvest), *sól* (sun), *týr* (the god Týr), *bjarkan* (birch), *maðr* (man), *lǫgr* (water), *ýr* (yew). — [Wikipedia: Rune poem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rune_poem)
- Rune poems: Old English (likely 7th c.; the manuscript burned in the 1731 Cotton fire and survives through Hickes's 1705 copy), Norwegian (a 17th-c. copy of a 13th-c. manuscript, in skaldic metre, "(rune name)(copula) X" plus a rhyming line), and Icelandic (oldest manuscript c. 1500, "the most systemized," with kennings and Latin glosses, and not standardized across manuscripts). — [Wikipedia: Rune poem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rune_poem)

**Anglo-Saxon Futhorc (expansion)**
- The *a*-rune split into ᚪ *āc* (oak), ᚫ *æsc* (ash) and ᚩ *ōs* (god), giving 26 runes, "to account for the new phoneme produced by the Ingvaeonic split of allophones of long and short a." ᛣ *calc* and ᚸ *gar* (spear) were modified from *cen* and *gyfu* when /k/ and /g/ palatalized. The script grew to 28 and then 33 (the article also says "up to 34", so its counts are internally inconsistent). — [Wikipedia: Anglo-Saxon runes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_runes)
- The 9th-century Thames scramasax (Seax of Beagnoth) is "the only complete alphabet" (28 runes). The Franks Casket (8th c.) mixes runes with Roman script. Missionaries brought Latin at the end of the 6th c.; "By the 8th century, they were eventually overtaken by the Old English Latin alphabet." Þorn and ƿynn were *adopted into the Latin alphabet*. Runes were very rare by 1066. — [Wikipedia: Anglo-Saxon runes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_runes)

**Bind runes**
- A bind rune is a "ligature of two or more runes" (Icelandic *bandrún*), usually sharing a vertical stroke. They are "extremely rare in Viking Age inscriptions" but common in Proto-Norse and medieval inscriptions, and on some runestones they "may have been ornamental and used to highlight the name of the carver." "Same-stave runes" share a long stemline that can become part of a picture, such as a ship's mast. — [Wikipedia: Bind rune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bind_rune)
- Modern use: the Bluetooth logo combines ᚼ and ᛒ (H B, for Harald Bluetooth). The Thor Steinar clothing logo, combining Tiwaz and Sowilo, "caused controversy" for resembling SS insignia. — [Wikipedia: Bind rune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bind_rune)
- Unicode treats bind runes as ligatures, not separate code points. — [Wikipedia: Elder Futhark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elder_Futhark)

**Norse attitudes to runes and magic (primary texts and scholarship)**
- Formula words on bracteates include *alu*, *laþu* and *laukaz*; their meanings are unclear (alu has been linked to "ale" and ritual drinking, laukaz to "leek" and fertility). — [Wikipedia: Elder Futhark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elder_Futhark)
- An *erilaz* is "a person versed in runes, including their magic applications". There are meaningless chants such as *tuwatuwa* and *g͡ag͡ag͡a*, and the Kylver stone has stacked Tiwaz/Ansuz runes said to invoke Týr and the Æsir. — [Wikipedia: Runic magic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runic_magic)
- *Sigrdrífumál*: "victory runes" carved on a sword hilt ("name Tyr twice"), ale-runes against bewitchment, *naudiz* on the fingernails and *laukaz* on the cup, plus birth-, wave-, branch-, speech- and thought-runes. — [Wikipedia: Runic magic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runic_magic)
- *Hávamál* Rúnatal (stanzas 139–146 per Wikipedia; editors often move st. 138): Odin hangs on a "windy tree" nine nights, wounded by a spear, "myself to myself," then takes up the runes "screaming." The Ljóðatal (18 charms, st. 147–165) mentions runes explicitly only in the 12th: "So do I write / and color the runes." — [Wikipedia: Hávamál](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C3%A1vam%C3%A1l)
- *Egil's Saga*: Egil cuts his hand, carves runes on a drinking horn and paints them with blood to expose poison. Whether blood-painting is "merely a literary invention" is uncertain. — [Wikipedia: Runic magic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runic_magic)
- *Egil's Saga*, the sick girl Helga: a rejected suitor carved runes on a whalebone in her bed and made her worse ("Ten spell-words writ wrongly"). Egil scrapes and burns the bone, recites a verse warning that no one should carve runes who cannot read them, then carves new healing runes. — [The Story of Egil Skallagrimsson, W. C. Green tr., Wikisource mirror](https://kwx.liehendr.com/content/wikisource_en_all_maxi_2022-09/A/Page%3AThe_Story_of_Egil_Skallagrimsson.djvu/190) (search snippets). He puts the runes under her pillow and she is healed; "The moral of the story is that runes and words can be dangerous in the wrong hands." — [National Museum of Denmark: Runic magic](https://en.natmus.dk/historical-knowledge/denmark/prehistoric-period-until-1050-ad/the-viking-age/religion-magic-death-and-rituals/runic-magic/)
- Curse runestones include Glavendrup (DR 209), Skjern (DR 81) and Tryggevælde (DR 230); Björketorp (DR 360) and Stentoften (DR 357) carry the phrase "runes of power." — [Wikipedia: Runic magic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runic_magic). The Glavendrup curse reads: "A warlock be he who damages(?) this stone or drags it (to stand) in memory of another," and the stone also says "Þórr hallow these runes." — [National Museum of Denmark](https://en.natmus.dk/historical-knowledge/denmark/prehistoric-period-until-1050-ad/the-viking-age/religion-magic-death-and-rituals/runic-magic/)
- **Scholarly skepticism**: "Runic magic seems more of a Medieval and Early Modern invention than a Viking-Age fact"; most inscriptions "contain more down-to-earth messages." Medieval rune-staffs with curses were waved at targets, and runes appear on church bells, thuribles and amulets, often in Christian contexts. — [National Museum of Denmark](https://en.natmus.dk/historical-knowledge/denmark/prehistoric-period-until-1050-ad/the-viking-age/religion-magic-death-and-rituals/runic-magic/). Wikipedia's *Runic magic* article itself flags many claims as "dubious" or "citation needed." — [Wikipedia: Runic magic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runic_magic)
- **Suppression precedent (Iceland)**: there were 120 witch trials from 1604 to 1720 and 22 confirmed executions from 1625 to 1685, mostly of men. A 17th-century Danish-imposed Witchcraft Act banned owning magic books, among other practices. Jón Rögnvaldsson was burned in 1625; the claim that "papers with mysterious signs" were found on him comes from a late, poorly sourced 19th-century writer. A 1655–56 case involved alleged "fart runes" (*fretrúnir*). Magical staves (*galdrastafir*) evolved from runes. — [Wikipedia: Witch trials in Iceland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witch_trials_in_Iceland); background at the [Museum of Icelandic Sorcery & Witchcraft](https://galdrasyning.is/en/galdrasyning-a-holmavik/). I found **no** source confirming a blanket legal ban on *runes as letters*; the laws targeted magic books and practice.

### Inferences
- **A precedent for a forbidden script.** Real history fits Auristar closely. Runes were displaced by a church-backed literate script (Latin) and kept a marginal afterlife in carving, charms and suspicion. Icelandic law punished *magic* (books, staves), not writing as such. A star city-state could likewise ban rune *casting* while scholars still read runes. That keeps the lore door open for archivists, inscriptions on old stones, and contraband.
- **A two-script ecology modelled on Tolkien and medieval Scandinavia.** After Tengwar became the main script, "the Cirth was mostly limited to carved inscriptions" ([Wikipedia: Cirth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cirth)). Medieval Norway likewise used runes on wooden sticks alongside Latin manuscripts. The star script can be the official, pen- and glass-friendly literate script, while runes survive as angular, carved, feared residue. Like þorn and wynn, a few rune-derived letters could be "defanged" and absorbed into the star script, a nice detail scholars in-world could argue about.
- **Medium for the star script.** The DDU visual brief names masterfully carved white stone and stained-glass domes of Starifjolden crystal. Stained glass is built from lead lines (came) enclosing coloured cells, so star-script glyphs built from *closed cells and continuous lines*, with no tiny counters, would translate directly into glass and inlay. That gives a medium-driven reason for a distinct look, just as wood grain is the classic story for runes. (This medium-to-glyph reasoning is my own extrapolation from the cited medium examples.)
- **Under-specification as a magic hook.** The Younger Futhark's "one rune, many sounds" ambiguity is a ready-made in-world explanation for why rune magic is dangerous: the same carved string can be read several ways, and Egil's "ten spell-words writ wrongly" shows a miscarving causing harm. A star script with one-glyph-one-sound precision can be framed in-world as the "safe," unambiguous script.
- **Real-world hazard.** Some rune shapes and bind-runes carry extremist associations (see the Thor Steinar case). Glyph and logo design should be screened against hate-symbol databases.

### Gaps
- Uppsala Runic Forum and Rundata (Scandinavian Runic-text Database) were not consulted directly. Scholarly runology (e.g., Michael Barnes, Terje Spurkland, R. I. Page) is referenced only second-hand through Wikipedia.
- I did not retrieve a direct, authoritative source for the precise number of runic inscriptions with explicit magic formulas, or for the Norwegian and Icelandic rune-poem stanza texts.
- No primary source confirmed a "17th-century ban on runes in Iceland"; this popular claim should be treated as unverified.
- The [ADL Hate Symbols database](https://www.adl.org/resources/hate-symbols/search) is the obvious screening source but was not fetched in this session.

## 3. How famous fictional scripts were built and why they work (Tengwar, Cirth, Dovahzul, Aurebesh, pIqaD, Kryptonian, Hylian, Tunic, Daedric)

### Takeaway
Fictional scripts that hold up under close reading (Tengwar, Cirth) encode sound systematically *and* have an in-world medium and history. Screen and game scripts that succeed visually (Dovahzul, pIqaD, Aurebesh) start from a strong visual concept tied to the culture: "carved into stone with a dragon's talon," "bladed weapons," a Eurostile-like sci-fi geometry. Many are just Latin ciphers (Aurebesh, Daedric, Twilight Princess Hylian, the Dovahzul alphabet), which is cheap and invites fan decoding but has no depth. Tunic shows a third route: a phonemic (not letter-by-letter) English script built to *resist* simple cipher-breaking, which turned decoding into a community event.

### Cited Findings
**Tolkien: Tengwar**
- Fëanor invented the Tengwar, based on Rúmil's *Sarati*, and coined *tengwa* for a letter representing a phoneme. Modes are orthographies for specific languages (Quenya, Sindarin, English, and the "mode of Beleriand" on the Doors of Durin). In *ómatehtar* modes vowels are diacritics (*tehtar*), which makes those modes abugidas. The One Ring inscription is Black Speech in Tengwar. — [Wikipedia: Tengwar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tengwar)
- Featural grid: four *témar* × six *tyeller*; bow-doubling, stem-raising and stem-shortening systematically mark voicing, fricatives and nasals. — [Wikipedia: Tengwar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tengwar)

**Tolkien: Cirth (adapted for carving)**
- "Semi-artificial," based on real runic alphabets. Many letters resemble historical runes, but their sound values mostly don't match, apart from a few vowels. Sound assignment is far more systematic: e.g., an extra stroke marks voicing. — [Wikipedia: Cirth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cirth)
- They were mostly used for names and brief memorials on wood, stone or metal, hence the angular shapes and straight lines. The Sindarin name *cirth* comes from the root *\*kir-*, "to cleave, to cut." Most letters are a vertical stem plus branches, and the side of a one-sided branch was originally not distinctive. — [Wikipedia: Cirth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cirth)
- Angerthas Daeron: a branch on the right = voiceless stop, moved left = spirant, an added stroke = voiced, branches on both sides = voice plus nasal, organized in series by place of articulation. Angerthas Moria (Dwarvish) introduced "unsystematic changes in value, as well as certain new cirth," and the Dwarves also used it with pens. The Mode of Erebor adds diacritics and appears in the Book of Mazarbul and on Balin's tomb. — [Wikipedia: Cirth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cirth)
- The runes in *The Hobbit* are "English runes" standing in for Dwarvish ones: the Old English Futhorc adapted orthographically to Modern English. — [Wikipedia: Cirth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cirth)

**Skyrim: Dovahzul (dragon language and alphabet)**
- Emil Pagliarulo (then Senior Designer) created the language after conversations with Todd Howard. "The visual language was Todd Howard's idea": he wanted it to look "carved into stone with a dragon's talon." Concept artist **Adam Adamowicz** designed and drew the symbols. The written script is "cuneiform-like … formed from letters that invoke the Dragon's fearsome form." — [Bethesda: How BGS made Skyrim's Dragon language](https://elderscrolls.bethesda.net/en-EU/news/how-bethesda-game-studios-made-skyrims-dragon-language)
- The language was primarily "a gameplay device for using the shouts". Shouts use one to three single-syllable words (the first is inhaled, the rest exhaled forcefully), inspired by the Weirding Module in Lynch's *Dune*. The theme song's lyrics had to rhyme in both English and Dragon. The vocabulary drew on Swedish, Danish, German and especially Old English (a *Beowulf* recording). Pagliarulo calls it "smoke and mirrors," built "to draw the player in and get them immersed." — [Bethesda](https://elderscrolls.bethesda.net/en-EU/news/how-bethesda-game-studios-made-skyrims-dragon-language)
- The alphabet has 34 symbols: 25 map directly to Latin letters and 9 "represent pairs" of letters. The snippet did not say which pairs; my unverified understanding is long vowels and diphthongs. Dragon text appears on dungeon walls and burial grounds as **Word Walls**. — [UESP: Lore:Dragon Alphabet](https://content3.m.uesp.net/wiki/Lore:Dragon_Alphabet) (search snippet; the page was Cloudflare-blocked)
- A secondary essay describes the visuals as Sumerian cuneiform combined with claw scratches. — [The Symbolic World essay](https://thesymbolicworld.com/content/the-logos-dragons-and-revelation-in-the-elder-scrolls-v-skyrim) (not a developer source). The original 2011 feature is [Game Informer: Skyrim's Dragon Shouts](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2011/01/20/skyrim-s-dragon-shouts) (not retrieved in full).

**Star Wars: Aurebesh**
- Joe Johnston designed the glyph shapes for the original trilogy (briefly seen in *Return of the Jedi* displays; a font called *Star Wars 76*). In the early 1990s Stephen Crane, art director at West End Games, copied them from screenshots, "standardized them based on shapes similar to Eurostile," and gave each a name and value; "Aurebesh" comes from *aurek* + *besh*. Letters correspond to English letters plus "certain English digraphs." — [Wikipedia: Aurebesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurebesh)
- Lucasfilm allowed it on condition that it be presented as *one of many* alphabets in the galaxy. It was used to render player characters' names in the RPG. The 2004 DVDs replaced Latin on-screen text with Aurebesh, it appears on Star Tours signage at Tokyo Disneyland, and Google Translate briefly offered it (Nov 2015–Feb 2016). — [Wikipedia: Aurebesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurebesh)

**Star Trek: Klingon pIqaD**
- Designed by Astra Image Corporation (often wrongly credited to Michael Okuda) as "merely decorative graphic elements, designed to simulate real writing". It was partly based on battlecruiser hull markings and Tibetan script, with sharp forms alluding to the Klingons' love of bladed weapons. — [Wikipedia: Klingon scripts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klingon_scripts)
- The later KLI pIqaD has 26 letters with a one-to-one grapheme–phoneme correspondence. An earlier Skybox set "did not map the Okrandian phonology," so it was never adopted. "The vast majority of Klingonists prefer Latin-based romanization." Unicode rejected the 1997 proposal in 2001 because "research showed almost no use of the script for communication"; it lives in the CSUR at U+F8D0–U+F8FF. — [Wikipedia: Klingon scripts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klingon_scripts)

**DC: Kryptonian**
- "For most of Superman's published history, Kryptonian writing was represented by random, alien-looking squiggles." In the 1970s E. Nelson Bridwell rationalized them into a 118-letter "Kryptonese," used until John Byrne's 1986 reboot. In 2000 DC introduced a transliteration alphabet renamed "Kryptonian." Linguistic anthropologist Dr. Christine Schreyer created the Kryptonian language for *Man of Steel* (2013), *BvS* (2016) and *Justice League* (2017). — [Wikipedia: Kryptonian language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kryptonian_language)

**Nintendo: Hylian scripts**
- Several unrelated "Hylian" scripts exist. Older ones (e.g., Ocarina of Time, and the Wind Waker script first shown in the Japanese manual) map to **Japanese kana**. *Twilight Princess* is generally cited as the first **English-letter** substitution cipher, and its official key came only with *Hyrule Historia* (Dec 2011). Because kana have no L/R distinction, translations often confuse L and R. — [Zelda Wiki: Hylian Language](https://zeldawiki.wiki/wiki/Hylian_Language); [dCode: Twilight Princess Hylian](https://www.dcode.fr/hylian-language-twilight-princess); [GlitterBerri: Writing Systems of Hyrule](https://glitterberri.com/hyrule-historia/writing-systems-of-hyrule/) (search snippets; pages blocked or not fetched)
- Fan scholars actively translate *Tears of the Kingdom*'s scripts. — [Nintendo Life feature](https://nintendolife.com/features/how-amateur-scholars-are-translating-zelda-tears-of-the-kingdoms-secret-language) (not fetched)

**Tunic (Isometricorp/Finji, 2022)**
- "the majority of the text is in a constructed writing system." Andrew Shouldice did not want it to be "a transliteration of English" because players would check for a simple cipher, as they had in *Fez*. He wanted "a sense of being presented with something that has meaning, but which was not understandable by the player," evoking his childhood experience of NES manuals he couldn't fully read, and hoped it would "encourage players to collaborate." — [Wikipedia: Tunic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunic_(video_game))
- Players nonetheless decoded it within about a month as **phonetic English**: glyphs combine a consonant and a vowel sound, and a connecting line runs through words. A hidden audio language ("Tuneic," by Kevin Regamey / PowerUp Audio) converts the phoneme glyphs into note sequences. — [NME](https://www.nme.com/news/a-tunic-player-has-translated-the-in-game-language-3197321); [GameRant](https://gamerant.com/tunic-game-language-translate-meaning/); [GamesRadar](https://www.gamesradar.com/players-of-last-years-best-zelda-like-are-still-trying-to-crack-its-secrets-but-its-creator-didnt-expect-them-to-dig-this-deep) (search summaries; full text not retrieved)

**Elder Scrolls: Daedric alphabet**
- A Latin-letter substitution script designed by Mark Jackson for *Battlespire*, named after the invading Daedra, and used heavily in *Morrowind* by the Dunmer and Daedra. Former developer Michael Kirkbride reportedly said X and Y are absent from in-game books because they didn't fit the page grid. — [UESP: Lore talk:Daedric Alphabet](https://en.uesp.net/wiki/Lore_talk:Daedric_Alphabet); [Imperial Library](https://www.imperial-library.info/?p=8435); [dCode: Daedric](https://www.dcode.fr/daedric-language) (search snippets; the "reportedly" is from the source)

### Inferences
- **What made them work visually**: one *physical* metaphor per culture. Dragon talon in stone gives Dovahzul; blades and hull markings give pIqaD; Eurostile sci-fi geometry gives Aurebesh. For DDU the two metaphors could be **runes as cut or gouged wood and stone** (Viking, forbidden, raw) and **the star script as constellation and stained glass** (points joined by lines, lead-came cells, radial symmetry), so the culture-clash is visible at a glance.
- **What made them work functionally**: Tolkien's scripts and KLI pIqaD map systematically to the language's phonology. Ciphers work for set dressing and fan decoding but break down when the language has sounds or digraphs English lacks; Dovahzul's 9 pair-glyphs and Aurebesh's digraph letters are patches for that. Lucasfilm's "one of many alphabets" condition is a good model for leaving room for regional variants.
- **Word walls → architecture.** Skyrim's Word Walls put the script on environmental architecture and tie it to the shout mechanic. The detail that reading a wall teaches a shout word is general game knowledge, not verified from a source in this session. DDU could mirror this: carved rune walls hidden or defaced in Auristar, and star-script inscriptions on domes and canals as civic or prayer texts. Whether reading runes is "dangerous" is a lore decision.
- **Decodability as engagement.** Hylian, Daedric and Tunic show that fans will decode any in-game script. If DDU text is a cipher of English, the community will solve it fast, so either embrace that (hide lore in it) or follow Tunic's phonemic route.

## 4. Neography design process and pitfalls

### Takeaway
Credible process: (1) settle the phonology and the script's function; (2) choose type, inventory and direction; (3) choose medium and tool and derive a small set of basic strokes; (4) optionally derive glyphs from pictograms or an *ancestor script* and simplify through repeated fast writing; (5) refine for cohesion and distinctness, then test at small size and in cursive. The pitfalls everyone names are Latin-cipher syndrome, lookalike or mirrored glyphs, decorative over-elaboration, and ignoring how speed and tools change letters over time. Sources disagree on whether ciphers are inherently bad.

### Cited Findings
**Process**
- Neography.info's three steps: (1) decide how the script works (character type, set, direction); (2) design the characters (aesthetic goal, optional *evolution method*, graphical method); (3) refine (cohesiveness, alignment, typography and calligraphy). — [Neography.info](https://neography.info/); [Create a script](https://neography.info/create-a-script/)
- Aesthetic goal: collect visual references, which "need not be scripts," and combine elements. Asemic (meaningless, text-like) sketches are a valid starting point. — [Neography.info: Create a script](https://neography.info/create-a-script/)
- Evolution method: assign a pictogram to each character and simplify it to an abstract symbol; "Pictograms are starting points, not end goals." — [Neography.info](https://neography.info/create-a-script/). "The letter *A* has its origins in a glyph representing the head of a bull." — [Wikibooks](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Conlang/Beginner/Writing)
- Graphical method: identify a small set of reusable basic strokes and "pick quick and convenient motions"; fewer, versatile strokes with contrasting geometry work best. — [Neography.info](https://neography.info/create-a-script/)
- Refine: simplify, reuse shapes and keep curves consistent, but don't reuse so heavily that reading suffers ("Letters like b, p, d, q make reading harder for people with dyslexia"). Align to consistent guidelines and midlines, keep widths proportional, and test with sample texts. — [Neography.info](https://neography.info/create-a-script/)
- Rosenfelder: "Elaborate letters are likely to be simplified." Write each letter repeatedly to see which simplifications emerge. Lowercase is a cursive simplification of ancient uppercase. "Keep the letters looking distinct," since lookalikes force users to add marks such as the slashed zero. — [Zompist LCK](https://www.zompist.com/kitlong.html)
- Make a cursive by writing each letter quickly and slurring it while keeping letters distinct. Find how small glyphs can get before details blur, which matters for fonts. Count the glyphs, because a large inventory needs more distinguishing detail per glyph. Avoid symbols that are very similar or simply rotated "unless the script is meant to be hard to read." — [Wikibooks](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Conlang/Beginner/Writing)
- Scripts drift and create historical spellings, and "you can add some interesting things by evolving your language." — [Conlang Guide](https://conlang-guide.bearblog.dev/deep-dives-writing-systems/)
- Peterson's *The Art of Language Invention* (Penguin, 2015) includes a writing-systems chapter. A reviewer notes it covers "the development of writing systems and the effects different writing implements crucially have on their evolution." — [Benung (Ayeri blog) AOLI posts](https://benung.nfshost.com/tag/aoli) (search snippet); [Penguin Random House listing](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/316207/the-art-of-language-invention-by-david-j-peterson/9780143126461/). Peterson says his naturalistic languages "evolve over time, and the evolution is simulated." — [Cleaver Magazine interview](https://cleavermagazine.com/brian-burmeister-interviews-david-j-peterson-author-of-the-art-of-language-invention)

**Pitfalls**
- Cipher-of-Latin: consider the phonology and the world's history so the script isn't a copy of Latin, and "avoid it being too English-y or Latin-y." — [Conlang Guide](https://conlang-guide.bearblog.dev/deep-dives-writing-systems/). Rosenfelder lists "an alphabet that looks too much like English, with extra apostrophes and marks" and "letters easily confused" as common mistakes. — [Zompist LCK](https://www.zompist.com/kitlong.html)
- **Contrasting view**: Neography.info's own demo is an English cipher with added CH/SH/TH/NG letters and one syllabogram, and it notes that "even a simple left-to-right cipher can be unique and beautiful." — [Neography.info](https://neography.info/create-a-script/)
- Real-world precedent on cipher detection: Tunic's designer avoided plain transliteration because players test for ciphers. — [Wikipedia: Tunic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunic_(video_game))
- Lookalike glyphs in practice: the sitelen pona font guidelines list confusable pairs (e.g., keep *seme* distinct from "?"; don't make circles so massive that *mi*, *sina*, *ona* and *lape* blur). — [sona.pona.la: sitelen pona font guidelines](https://sona.pona.la/wiki/sitelen_pona_font_guidelines)
- Real scripts evolve by *reduction and specialization*, not only invention. Examples: cuneiform's 1,500→600 signs and five basic wedges ([Wikipedia: Cuneiform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuneiform)), Younger Futhark's 24→16 and the staveless runes that "lack certain strokes" ([Wikipedia: Younger Futhark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Younger_Futhark)), and the Cirth's unsystematic Dwarvish changes ([Wikipedia: Cirth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cirth)).

### Inferences
- **A recommended pipeline for DDU** (synthesis of the above):
  1. Fix the phonology and the romanization first (Q5).
  2. **Runes**: either reuse historical Futhark-like shapes with *reassigned* values (as Tolkien did with the Cirth, which avoids a literal Younger Futhark) or derive them from a fictional proto-script. Give them carving logic: stem plus branches, no horizontals, no curves. Simulate history by producing an older "full" row and a later reduced row (forbidden-era "short-twig" scratches vs. monumental "long-branch" stones).
  3. **Star script**: in-world, derive it *from* the runes (a reform by star-priests or scholars) or from astronomical notation, such as dots for stars and lines for constellations. Pick a tool (stylus on wax, pen on vellum, chisel on white marble, lead came in glass) and a stroke set of about 5–7 primitives: a dot, a ray, an arc, a ring, a 7-point star motif (Auristar's emblem), and a baseline or "horizon" connector, possibly echoing Ogham's stemline or Tunic's connecting line.
  4. Simplify by repeated fast writing to get a cursive or "scribal" hand distinct from the monumental and glass forms, then check at UI sizes.
  5. Run a confusability pass: no glyph should be a pure rotation or mirror of another, especially given the dyslexia note.
- Because the star script should read as the anti-rune, it can deliberately use the features runes avoid: curves, horizontals, closed counters and radial symmetry. That is a medium-justified visual opposition.

## 5. Romanization and orthography design

### Takeaway
Start from the phoneme inventory and spell it as plainly as possible. Use either digraphs *or* diacritics consistently, never decoratively. Avoid apostrophes unless they mean a specific sound such as a glottal stop. Expect English readers to apply English values: Tolkien's hard *c* is still misread, and Okrand's capitals were partly there to steer actors. For a Norse flavor, scholarly normalized Old Norse (þ, ð, æ, ø/œ, ǫ, acute for length, j = /j/) is the authentic template. An ASCII fallback (th, dh, ae, oe, double vowels) is practically necessary for fans, search and game strings.

### Cited Findings
**General principles**
- Transliteration is "a one-to-one mapping of characters"; transcription records sound. Most romanizations for casual readers "lean more towards transcription". "Pure transcriptions are generally not possible," so systems compromise. Some reversible systems allow "an irreversible simplified version." ASCII-only schemes such as Harvard-Kyoto use capitalization and doubled letters instead of diacritics. Hepburn (phonetic, aimed at Anglo-American readers) is easier for English readers to pronounce than Nihon-shiki or Kunrei. Competing systems have caused "great reduplication of names" (the Tchaikovsky example). — [Wikipedia: Romanization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization)
- Rosenfelder: "Work on the phonemes, then find a way to spell them in a straightforward fashion"; "I don't recommend trying to be very creative here," since unusual spellings often disguise an English-like sound system. Tolkien's Quenya is the model, yet his hard *c*/*g* is still mispronounced. — [Zompist LCK](https://www.zompist.com/kitlong.html)
- Rosenfelder on diacritics and apostrophes: "never use a diacritical mark without giving it a specific meaning, preferably one which it retains in all uses". Apostrophes have contradictory uses across languages, so they "end up suggesting nothing at all to the reader". "Please don't browse the Unicode catalog as if all the characters were just decoration." He dislikes digraphs and prefers Czech-style háčeks (č, š). — [Zompist LCK](https://www.zompist.com/kitlong.html)
- Rosenfelder suggests different romanization styles per in-world nation to give each a distinct feel: *Ďarcaln* vs. *Dhârkalen* name the same place. — [Zompist LCK](https://www.zompist.com/kitlong.html)
- A romanization is not an in-universe script. Dothraki is written in Latin letters for convenience, but no one in-world writes it that way. — [Conlang Guide](https://conlang-guide.bearblog.dev/deep-dives-writing-systems/)
- The UCL course homework: choose a representation for each phoneme, digraphs and diacritics allowed, with Franklin's phonetic alphabet, Esperanto orthography and Klingon as models. — [UCL PLIN0068](https://www.homepages.ucl.ac.uk/~ucjtudo/assets/teaching/PLIN0068/4_writing_systems.html)

**Norse letters: the normalized Old Norse template**
- 19th-century scholars built a standardized normalization "primarily based on the so-called First Grammatical Treatise" that marks vowel length (acute: á, é, í, ó, ú, ý) and umlaut. It uses þ (/θ/), ð (/ð/), æ, œ, ø and ǫ, with j for /j/. Manuscripts "did not employ a unified system of spelling". — [Wikipedia: Old Norse orthography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse_orthography)
- Practical precedent for fallback: "the use of ö instead of ǫ" became widespread because ǫ was missing from early electronic character sets (ǫ was added to Unicode in 1991). — [Wikipedia: Old Norse orthography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse_orthography)
- Modern Icelandic follows the normalization but reflects later sound changes (ǫ→ö, œ→æ, final k/t→g/ð, so *mik*→*mig*; *Baldr*→*Baldur*). Danish and Swedish runestone scholarship sometimes uses an Old East Norse variant (æi for ei). — [Wikipedia: Old Norse orthography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse_orthography)

**How franchises romanize**
- **Dothraki (Peterson)**: digraphs ⟨ch⟩ /tʃ/, ⟨kh⟩ /x/, ⟨sh⟩ /ʃ/, ⟨th⟩ /θ/, ⟨zh⟩ /ʒ/; ⟨j⟩ = /dʒ/, ⟨y⟩ = /j/. Geminate digraphs are written ⟨kkh⟩, ⟨tth⟩, ⟨ssh⟩, ⟨zzh⟩. Repeated vowels are *separate syllables*: *khaleesi* [ˈxa.le.e.si]. Spelling "had to match the uses already put down in the books," so ⟨p⟩, ⟨b⟩ and ⟨u⟩ survive only in Martin's names; the language "had to be easily pronounceable or learnable by the actors." The Dothraki "do not have a writing system." — [Wikipedia: Dothraki language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dothraki_language)
- **Klingon (Okrand)**: case-sensitive. Capitals mostly mark sounds "not natively found in American English" (D, Q, S retroflex or uvular; H), partly to help actors. The only minimal pair is Q/q (*Qat* "be popular" vs. *qat* "accompany"). ʼ is a *letter* (glottal stop), like the Hawaiian ʻokina. Multigraphs are ch, gh, ng and tlh. Capital *I* is confusable with *l* in sans-serif fonts, so serif fonts (Courier) are traditional. — [Wikipedia: Klingon language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klingon_language)
- **Apostrophes in fantasy**: Tor.com notes they're often attributed to Anne McCaffrey, though Lovecraft used them earlier. Tolkien used acute and circumflex accents rather than apostrophes in his conlangs. — [Tor.com: Apostrophes in SF/F names](https://tor.com/2013/08/27/apostrophes-in-science-fiction-and-fantasy-names). One conlanger calls them so clichéd as to be avoided and suggests a diaeresis for vowel hiatus instead. — [Crafting Languages: fantasy apostrophe](https://craftinglanguages.substack.com/p/fantasy-apostrophe)
- **Dovahzul's alphabet** includes 9 glyphs that "represent pairs" of Latin letters, so its romanization uses letter pairs that each have a single glyph. — [UESP: Lore:Dragon Alphabet](https://content3.m.uesp.net/wiki/Lore:Dragon_Alphabet) (search snippet). I could not confirm which pairs they are (e.g., doubled vowels such as "aa"); see Gaps.

### Inferences
- **A recommended two-tier romanization for DDU** (synthesis):
  - *Canonical (lore bible, maps, codex)*: normalized-Old-Norse style with þ, ð, æ, ø, acute accents for long vowels, and **j = /j/** always. That's authentic and pretty, and it matches what Norse-literate fans expect.
  - *ASCII fallback (UI strings, file names, community chat, hashtags)*: th for þ, dh for ð (or plain d), ae for æ, oe for ø, and doubled vowels or bare vowels for long vowels. Publish the mapping once and keep it reversible (Harvard-Kyoto-style logic).
  - **å** is *not* Old Norse (it comes from later Scandinavian spelling; general knowledge, not verified in this session). Using it signals "modern Swedish/Norwegian" more than "Viking," so reserve it for a deliberately different in-world dialect if at all. That follows Rosenfelder's one-style-per-nation advice: e.g., rune-region names in Norse-normalized style, Auristar names in a cleaner, more "Latinate/celestial" style.
  - **Apostrophes**: give them exactly one meaning (e.g., a glottal stop or a morpheme boundary in compounds) or ban them.
  - English-reader traps: ⟨j⟩ will be read /dʒ/ and ⟨c⟩ as /s/ before e or i. Use ⟨k⟩, not ⟨c⟩, and accept that ⟨j⟩ needs a pronunciation guide, or use ⟨y⟩ in the ASCII tier.
- For a community writing in English, consistency beats authenticity: one published table, a pronunciation guide, and a rule that the canonical and ASCII forms map one-to-one.
- Font implication: if ⟨I⟩ vs ⟨l⟩, ⟨þ⟩ vs ⟨p⟩ or ⟨ð⟩ vs ⟨d⟩ might confuse readers in the UI typeface, choose a UI Latin font that clearly distinguishes them (the Klingon lesson).

### Gaps

- I did not research how Norse-themed games (God of War, Assassin's Creed Valhalla, Hellblade, Valheim) romanize Old Norse names; no sources were gathered.

## 6. Fonts, encoding, ligatures and in-game use

### Takeaway
For a constructed script you either (a) map glyphs onto **Latin code points** (a "cipher font," optionally with ligatures that turn typed Latin words into glyphs), which is easiest for game pipelines and localization, or (b) encode them in the **Private Use Area**, ideally following or registering with the **ConScript/Under-ConScript Unicode Registry**, which is "correct" text but fragile across fonts and software. Tools range from Calligraphr (handwriting scans, quick) through FontStruct (easy) to FontForge and Glyphs (full OpenType control). Game engines support only a subset of OpenType: Unity TextMesh Pro handles kern, liga, mark and mkmk, so complex contextual shaping should be avoided or baked in.

### Cited Findings
**Encoding: PUA and the ConScript registries**
- The BMP Private Use Area is U+E000–U+F8FF (6,400 code points), and planes 15–16 are almost entirely private use. PUA code points have no standard meaning ("Character semantics are left to private agreement"), and "A user may see one private character from an installed font where a different one was intended." — [Wikipedia: Private Use Areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_Use_Areas)
- The ConScript Unicode Registry (CSUR, led by John Cowan and Michael Everson) coordinates PUA blocks for constructed scripts. The Under-ConScript registry (UCSUR) holds submitted scripts not yet in CSUR so "nobody steps on each other's toes." Example allocations: Tengwar E000, Cirth E080, D'ni E830, Aurebesh E890, Klingon F8D0. Withdrawn scripts that gained official Unicode blocks include Shavian, Deseret and the Phaistos Disc. Everson has "very little time to update the CSUR." — [Kreative Korp: UCSUR](https://www.kreativekorp.com/ucsur/)
- Tengwar: Everson proposed it for Unicode in 1997; a Supplementary Multilingual Plane range was tentatively placed on the 2023 roadmap but later deleted. In CSUR it sits at U+E000–U+E07F, and "the encoding order was never standardized, with two competing proposals." — [Wikipedia: Tengwar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tengwar)
- Klingon was rejected by Unicode in 2001 for lack of real communicative use. — [Wikipedia: Klingon scripts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klingon_scripts)
- sitelen pona (Toki Pona) was added to UCSUR on 26 Aug 2021. Rebecca Bettencourt (jan Lepeka), "who is also in charge of UCSUR," maintains the Fairfax font family covering all UCSUR scripts. — [sona.pona.la: Fonts](https://sona.pona.la/wiki/font) (search snippets)

**Ligature-driven input (the practical pattern)**
- sitelen pona fonts use glyph substitution so typed ASCII words display as glyphs ("toki" typed shows the *toki* glyph), letting people write "without a separate input method." UCSUR code points make fonts compatible with established input methods. Some fonts ship parallel builds (UCSUR plus Latin ligatures, or UCSUR only). Contextual ligatures auto-fill extension lines between start and end characters. Glyphs are centred on a midline, not a baseline. Making components align is better than pre-merging every combination, which "would result in a big font file and a lot of work." — [sona.pona.la: font guidelines](https://sona.pona.la/wiki/sitelen_pona_font_guidelines); [sona.pona.la: Fonts](https://sona.pona.la/wiki/font)

**Font tools**
- FontForge (powerful), FontStruct (easier) and Calligraphr are the tools the UCL conlang course suggests. — [UCL PLIN0068](https://www.homepages.ucl.ac.uk/~ucjtudo/assets/teaching/PLIN0068/4_writing_systems.html)
- FontForge: "FontForge cannot create a nice ligature for you". Build Composite inserts component references, which you unlink and refine. Name ligature glyphs by component (e.g., `longs_longs_l`) so FontForge can infer them. An unencoded ligature can "use a code point in the private use area". Ligatures go in a Ligature Substitution lookup bound to a feature (e.g., 'liga'), script and language. Caveat: "OpenType engines will only apply features which they believe are appropriate for the current script." — [FontForge tutorial: ligatures and lookups](https://fontforge.org/docs/tutorial/editexample4.html)
- Calligraphr: the free plan allows 75 characters per font, 2 variants per character for randomization, and **no ligatures**. Pro allows 600 characters, 15 variants and ligatures (shown as MXN 180/month or MXN 140/month for 6 months; price is localized). Output is a standard TTF/OTF that stays usable after the subscription ends. — [Calligraphr pricing](https://www.calligraphr.com/en/pricing/)

**Game engine (Unity TextMesh Pro as the documented example)**
- The TMP Font Asset Creator accepts **Unicode Range (Hex)** lists (e.g., `20-7E,A0,2026`), custom decimal ranges, custom characters or characters from a file. "The characters in a font file aren't included in the font Asset automatically." SDF32 is recommended for "fonts with complex or small characters". Styling for SDF fonts lives in the shader. Large sets can be split across main and fallback assets. GPOS-stored kerning is "not supported by FreeType, the font engine used by TextMesh Pro." Assets go in a Resources folder. — [Unity TMP: Font Asset Creator](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.textmeshpro@4.0/manual/FontAssetsCreator.html)
- TMP supports the "liga" feature, toggled per text object under Extra Settings → Font Features. — [Unity TMP: Text objects](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.textmeshpro@4.0/manual/TMPObjects.html). The TextCore OTL feature enum lists only **kern, liga, mark, mkmk**. — [Unity TextCore OTL_FeatureTag](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.textmeshpro@4.0/api/UnityEngine.TextCore.OTL_FeatureTag.html). Unity states it has "limited support for OpenType Font Features" and one bug report notes ligatures breaking under the Uppercase style. — [Unity Issue Tracker](https://issuetracker.unity3d.com/issues/textmeshpro-characters-are-on-top-of-each-other-when-text-has-a-thai-font) (search snippet; exact issue page uncertain). Unity's direction is to fold TMP into TextCore, whose FontEngine "do[es] have support for almost all OpenType features but will require newer versions of the package." — [Unity Discussions: Future of Text at Unity](https://discussions.unity.com/t/future-of-text-at-unity/898971)

### Inferences
- **Recommended production approach for DDU** (synthesis):
  - **Cipher-plus-ligature font for UI and marketing**: map star-script glyphs onto the ASCII romanization so designers and localizers type readable strings. Use 'liga' only for digraph letters (th→þ-glyph, long vowels) and ornamental combinations, and avoid 'calt'-dependent shaping, since TMP supports only kern/liga/mark/mkmk.
  - **PUA build for lore-community text**: if fans will *type* the script, also publish a PUA/UCSUR-style mapping, ideally registered with UCSUR to avoid collisions, and a parallel build like the sitelen pona fonts. Document the mapping publicly, since PUA has "no standard meaning."
  - **Runes**: real runes already have a Unicode block (U+16A0–U+16FF; [Wikipedia: Elder Futhark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elder_Futhark)). If DDU runes reuse historical shapes with new values, a custom font over that block risks confusing fans who know real runes, so a cipher font or a separate PUA range is cleaner.
  - **Art and architecture**: carved stone, stained glass and environmental decals don't need fonts. Draw these from a master glyph sheet (monumental form), keep the font for UI (scribal form), and keep both derived from the same stroke primitives for coherence.
  - **Small-size tests**: run the Wikibooks small-size test at actual UI sizes and use SDF32 for detailed glyphs. Radial star glyphs with fine rays will blur first.
- Calligraphr is a fast way to prototype a hand-drawn "scribe" font, but its free tier's 75-character cap and lack of ligatures make Pro or FontForge necessary for production.
