## Q1. How do Tolkien, Peterson, Biblaridion, Artifexian, Rosenfelder and others describe the diachronic workflow? Which sound changes are "natural", and how many layers or epochs are recommended?

### Takeaway
Every expert source describes the same core workflow. Design (or sketch) a proto-language and its lexicon. Then apply an ordered list of regular, category-wide sound changes to every word, layer grammar change and borrowing on top, and repeat per daughter or dialect. Peterson adds one point the others do not stress: the *sound profile of a contact language* (substrate or superstrate) should steer which changes you pick. No source gives a fixed number of epochs. Rosenfelder's rule of thumb is to keep adding changes until every word in your sample has been altered, and to calibrate against real time depths (Latin to French, about 2,000 years; Old to Modern German, about 1,000 years).

### Cited Findings

**General principles of the method**
- Sound change targets sounds, not words: "it's sounds that change, not words," and when a sound changes "all words which include those sounds are affected." — [OUP Blog, "Linguistic necromancy" (Proto-Germanic reconstruction)](https://blog.oup.com/2014/10/linguistic-necromancy-reconstruction-proto-germanic/)
- Rosenfelder (Language Construction Kit) says sound change is "almost completely regular." A change that starts sporadically "tends to keep on hitting more and more words, and generally it ends up getting them all." — [zompist.com, search snippet](https://www.zompist.com/sounds.htm)
- The Wikibooks Conlang "Common sound changes" page recommends two approaches. The first is to draft the target daughter phonology, then choose changes that move the proto-language toward it and apply them to the whole dictionary. The second is to pick changes at random for "less control and more surprises." Rules are written X > Y / Z_ with # for a word boundary. — [Wikibooks: Conlang/Intermediate/History/Common sound changes](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Conlang/Intermediate/History/Common_sound_changes)
- The same page gives several realism notes. Sound change "has no memory": a sound changes wherever its environment occurs, whatever its history. Grammar can block a change; in Brazilian Portuguese, final /r/ drops in infinitives but not in nouns. Frequent words are more susceptible to change. When a change makes two words collide, four outcomes are possible: homophony resolved by context, merged meanings, one word resisting the change, or one word being replaced by another construction. — [Wikibooks: Common sound changes](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Conlang/Intermediate/History/Common_sound_changes)
- The TV Tropes "So You Want To: Create a Language Family" guide likewise recommends building a proto-language first, then using its daughters "as new proto-languages that diverge into several new languages." This is the recursive family-tree method. — [TV Tropes, search snippet](https://www.tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/SoYouWantTo/CreateALanguageFamily)
- One Wikibooks conlang page warns that inventing the descendant first and then a proto-language is "much harder because you will have to work backwards." It names Zompist's SCA as "the original and most famous sound change applier." — [Wikibooks Conlang/Intermediate/History (mirror), search snippet](https://kwx.liehendr.com/content/wikibooks_en_all_maxi_2021-03/A/Conlang/Intermediate/History)

**Rosenfelder (zompist) on choosing changes and how many**
- Rosenfelder points to his *Language Construction Kit* list of common sound changes (p. 169, ed. 1.2): "You won't go wrong with any of them." — [Ask Zompist: Common sound changes (2019)](https://zompist.wordpress.com/2019/01/26/ask-zompist-common-sound-changes/)
- He makes four further points in the same post:
  - Lenitions "occur just about all over," and consonant-cluster simplification is common.
  - Changes should hit *categories* of sounds (e.g., all voiced consonants between vowels), not single phonemes.
  - Chain shifts are possible, especially among vowels, as in the Great Vowel Shift.
  - Neighboring unrelated languages can share changes through areal diffusion: French and German both developed ü, ö and uvular R, and Vietnamese developed tone like Chinese.
  — [Ask Zompist](https://zompist.wordpress.com/2019/01/26/ask-zompist-common-sound-changes/)
- On naturalness he advises picking changes you like that "twist the source words in an interesting way." He also says: "You can't really criticize a sound change for being weird, especially if at least one natlang does it!" — [Ask Zompist](https://zompist.wordpress.com/2019/01/26/ask-zompist-common-sound-changes/)
- On how many changes: stop "When your sound changes affect every word in your sample." Calibrate time depth against Latin to French (about 2,000 years) or Old to Modern German (about 1,000 years). He notes that written French roughly represents the 12th-century spoken language. — [Ask Zompist](https://zompist.wordpress.com/2019/01/26/ask-zompist-common-sound-changes/)
- *Advanced Language Construction* (the LCK sequel) reportedly adds material on where in a society sound changes start, how they spread, and how morphosyntax changes over time. — [zompist.com, search snippet](https://www.zompist.com/lckbook.html)
- For deeper background Rosenfelder recommends Theodora Bynon's and R. L. Trask's books, both titled *Historical Linguistics*. — [zompist.com/sounds.htm, search snippet](https://www.zompist.com/sounds.htm)

**The common "natural" sound changes, with examples**
- Wikibooks lists these core changes:
  - **Assimilation** ("by far the most important sound change"): e.g., Latin *ad-* + *similare* gives *assimilation*.
  - **Lenition**: voicing, then spirantization, then loss, especially between vowels; chains like /t/ > [d] > ..., and /k/ > ∅.
  - **Palatalization**: /tj/ > [tʃ], /sj/ > [ʃ], /hj/ > [ç]; English *-tion*.
  - **Velarization**.
  - **Monophthongization**: French *ai*, *eau* > [ɛ], [o].
  - **Nasalization**, which becomes phonemic if the nasal is later lost (French *maison*).
  - **Rhotacism**: Latin intervocalic s > z > r, giving *flos/floris* alternations, later regularized (*honor* for older *honos*).
  — [Wikibooks: Common sound changes](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Conlang/Intermediate/History/Common_sound_changes)
- That page does *not* cover apocope, metathesis, breaking, compensatory lengthening or fortition. It notes that SCAs "struggle with syllable-specific changes like syncope and multi-syllable environments like umlaut." — [Wikibooks: Common sound changes](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Conlang/Intermediate/History/Common_sound_changes)
- Index Diachronica is a large compiled list of *attested* sound changes from many languages. chridd's version adds a Unicode PDF, HTML, a machine-readable format and a search tool that queries by IPA segment or feature class (e.g., [+labial], [-voice]). Current version is 10.2, last modified 2022-01-29. Caveats: some source links are broken, the preface is out of date, and the maintainer does not hold all the sources. — [Index Diachronica (chridd)](https://chridd.nfshost.com/diachronica/)

**Tolkien: Quenya and Sindarin from a common ancestor**
- Letter 144 (to Naomi Mitchison, 25 April 1954), as summarized by Tolkien Gateway: Quenya was grammatically inspired by Latin, Finnish and Greek, and Sindarin had "a linguistic character similar to British-Welsh." — [Tolkien Gateway: Letter 144, search snippet](https://tolkiengateway.net/wiki/Letter_144)
- A secondary excerpt quotes the same letter calling Quenya a kind of "Elven-latin," with Finnish and Greek named as sources of "phonaesthetic" pleasure. The search tool flagged the excerpt as partly garbled. — [Scribd excerpt of a Tolkien study, search snippet](https://www.scribd.com/document/369389999/Elvish)
- A 1955 draft letter to David Masson says Sindarin was "deliberately composed to resemble (with a difference) Welsh." — [Tolkien Gateway: David Masson 1955, search snippet](https://tolkiengateway.net/wiki/David_Masson_1955)
- Sources conflict on Quenya's phonology. Tolkien Gateway says its phonology and grammar are most influenced by Finnish. A Wikipedia mirror says its pronunciation is based more on Latin than Finnish, with elements of both. — [Tolkien Gateway: Quenya](https://tolkiengateway.net/wiki/Quenya); contrast [Wikipedia (mirror): Languages constructed by Tolkien](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Entish)
- Eldamo (Paul Strack's lexicon) catalogs Sindarin's derivation as discrete, ordered, labeled "phonetic developments." Examples, stage-tagged OS. (Old Sindarin) vs S. (Sindarin):
  - OS. final i-diphthongs became long [ī] in polysyllables. — [Eldamo](https://eldamo.org/content/words/word-71909447.html)
  - S. [ē], [ō] became [ī], [ū]. — [Eldamo](https://eldamo.org/content/words/word-3889365613.html)
  - S. short [a], [o], [u] became [e], [œ], [y] before [i] ("i-fronting," the 2nd stage of i-affection, following Bertrand Bellet's 2005 *Vowel Affection in Sindarin and Noldorin*). — [Eldamo](https://eldamo.org/content/words/word-3958031275.html)
  - S. short [e], [o] became [i], [u] in the syllable before final [i]. — [Eldamo](https://eldamo.org/content/words/word-2646655607.html)
  - S. final [i] intruded into the preceding syllable, [-VCi] > [-ViC]. — [Eldamo](https://eldamo.org/content/words/word-2399289739.html)
  - S. final [i], [u] generally vanished (apocope). — [Eldamo](https://eldamo.org/content/words/word-1815401039.html)
  - S. [ɣ] vocalized to [i] before [l r m n]. — [Eldamo](https://eldamo.org/content/words/word-539122737.html)
- Eldamo's overview says i-affection is "the main process behind Sindarin plural nouns and adjectives," affects the present tense too, and splits into internal i-affection, final i-affection and final i-intrusion. Diphthong shifts made actual plural forms vary "considerably." — [Eldamo overview entry, search snippet](https://eldamo.org/content/words/word-2933767401.html)

**Peterson: High Valyrian to Astapori/Meereenese, and the Ghiscari layer**
- Peterson's "State of Valyrian" post gives his reasoning:
  - Ghiscari culture was displaced by a Valyrian ruling class, so "the result ends up being a Valyrian language grammatically, but with a lot of Ghiscari vocabulary." Old words survived where the new language lacked an equivalent, and among people never formally taught High Valyrian.
  - A language takes about "three generations" to disappear from a single family.
  - Astapori, Yunkish and Meereenese are dialects of one "Ghiscari Valyrian." Astapori's changes from High Valyrian "aren't that drastic."
  - Meereenese is most divergent because Meereen is the largest city with the biggest lower class: "It's the same language, but it's developed its own distinct character."
  - Isolated Dragonstone kept High Valyrian conservative.
  - Faster change comes from contact with other languages and from distance from Valyria.
  — [dothraki.com: The State of Valyrian (2014)](https://dothraki.com/2014/05/the-state-of-valyrian/)
- Per interview summaries, Peterson began with High Valyrian, built a sound profile of Ghiscari from the Ghiscari names in the books, and let that profile decide which sound changes to apply. He likened the result to Italian vs. Latin. Meereenese shares most grammar with Astapori but has more Ghiscari loans and "sounds very different." — [Little White Lies interview, search snippet](https://lwlies.com/articles/how-do-you-create-a-real-fake-language/); [Den of Geek, search snippet](https://www.denofgeek.com/us/tv/game-of-thrones/280461/game-of-thrones-high-valyrian-david-j-peterson)
- dothraki.com posts (search snippets; exact posts not verified) describe Astapori vs. High Valyrian:
  - All long vowels and most diphthongs were lost.
  - j is "pretty much always zh," and Astapori has a dh [ð] that High Valyrian lacks.
  - Most of the case system was dropped; word order tends to SVO; there are two genders instead of four.
  — [dothraki.com, search snippets](https://dothraki.com/2014/05/the-state-of-valyrian/)
- Peterson's *Art of Language Invention* (Penguin, 2015) is described as the first mass-market text on language construction. A newer edition adds a chapter on phrases: word order, negation, questions, pragmatics, relativization and subordination. — [Penguin Random House listing, search snippet](https://penguinrandomhouse.com/books/isbn/9780143126461); [Fiat Lingua PDF, 2025, search snippet](https://fiatlingua.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/fl-00010C-00.pdf)

**Biblaridion**
- Biblaridion's tutorial series is titled "How to Make a Language," not "Making a Language." Its tutorial proto-language is Proto-Simātsan, and viewers use it as the base for deriving descendants. — [r/conlangs FAQ thread (redlib mirror), search snippet](https://redlib.nohost.network/r/conlangs/comments/18l56i4/faq_small_discussions_20231218_to_20231231)

### Inferences
- **Recommended epoch structure for Norsund**, modeled on Tolkien's tagged stages (Primitive > Common Eldarin > Old Sindarin > Sindarin) and on Peterson's classical > dialect split:
  - **(0) Proto/Old Norsund**, the settlers' tongue.
  - **(1) Early Norsund**: settlement-era innovations such as umlaut, syncope and final-vowel loss.
  - **(2) Kedre-contact Norsund**: conquest-era borrowing and some grammatical simplification.
  - **(3) Modern Norsund**, split into (3a) rural or island dialects and (3b) the prestige city dialect.
  Each stage is just an ordered rule block in an SCA. Rosenfelder's "until every word in the sample is touched" test can be applied per stage.
- **Use Peterson's "contact profile steers sound changes" trick, inverted.** He let Ghiscari sounds pick the Valyrian changes. Let Kedre's (Latinate) sound profile pick some city-dialect changes: vowel-final words preserved or restored in prestige speech, intervocalic lenition, lost clusters. That makes the canal-city speech sound "Kedre-touched" with no copied Italian words.
- Peterson's choice to make the largest city with the biggest lower class the *most* divergent cuts against the intuition that the prestige city is conservative. Both options are defensible. The capital's dialect can be innovative in pronunciation (urban change spreading outward, cf. Rosenfelder's areal diffusion) while its written H-register stays archaic (see Q5).
- **Time depth:** if Old Norsund arrived roughly 1,000 years before "now," aim for Old-to-Modern-German-scale change (Rosenfelder's benchmark): readers should recognize cognates but not read the old texts unaided.

### Gaps
- Peterson's actual sound-change list for High Valyrian to Astapori/Meereenese was not found in fetched sources. Only the qualitative outcomes above (vowel-length loss, j > zh, case and gender reduction) are sourced.
- The text of *The Art of Language Invention*'s evolution material could not be fetched or verified.
- No primary Biblaridion or Artifexian video content (episode lists, transcripts, recommended changes) could be retrieved. Their specific workflow advice is unverified here.
- Ardalambion (Helge Fauskanger) failed with a TLS error, so his summary of Common Eldarin > Sindarin (voiceless-stop lenition, the mutations) is not cited.
- The frequently quoted full wording of Letter 144 was not verified verbatim: "composed on a Latin basis with two other (main) ingredients that happen to give me 'phonaesthetic' pleasure: Finnish and Greek," and the Sindarin changes "deliberately devised to give it a linguistic character very like (though not identical with) British-Welsh." Check against *The Letters of J.R.R. Tolkien* before quoting.
- Classic Sindarin plural examples (adan/edain, orod/ered) were not confirmed from a fetched page, though they are consistent with the Eldamo i-affection entries above.

## Q2. How do grammaticalization and analogy create believable irregular forms (strong/weak verbs, umlaut plurals), and how do you keep irregularity intentional?

### Takeaway
Irregularity in natural languages is mostly the *residue of regular sound change*. Umlaut, syncope and final-vowel loss erase the conditioning suffix and leave a vowel alternation behind. Analogy then *tidies some paradigms but not others*, with the most frequent words usually resisting. Grammaticalization supplies new affixes from old words (Latin *cantare habeo* > Romance future; Norse *sik* > mediopassive *-sk*). To keep irregularity intentional, generate it through the pipeline and then decide case by case which forms analogy levels.

### Cited Findings
- **Sturtevant's paradox:** "sound change is regular but produces irregularity, while analogy is irregular but produces regularity." — [Wikipedia: Analogical change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analogical_change)
- Analogical change is when a linguistic sign "is changed in either form or meaning" to match another item. Examples:
  - **Leveling:** Old English past singular *ceas* and plural *curon* collapsed into one Modern English past, *chose*.
  - **Four-part proportional analogy:** inarticulate : articulate :: inflammable : *flammable*.
  - **Maintenance:** the /w/ in *swollen* was kept by analogy with *swell* but lost in *sword*.
  - **Contamination:** *femelle* > *female* after *male*.
  - **Hypercorrection:** *autor* > *author* after *throne*.
  — [Wikipedia: Analogical change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analogical_change)
- Rhotacism in Latin produced *flos / floris*-type alternations that were later partly regularized (e.g., *honor* for *honos*). This is the classic sound-change-creates, analogy-repairs sequence. — [Wikibooks: Common sound changes](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Conlang/Intermediate/History/Common_sound_changes)
- **Old Norse irregularity produced by umlaut:**
  - i-umlaut fronted back vowels before *i/j*: *menn* 'men' < *\*manniz*; *mæla* 'speak' < *\*mālijan*.
  - u-umlaut rounded vowels before *u/w*: *lǫnd* 'lands' < *\*landu*; *skjǫldr* 'shield' < *\*skelduz* (breaking plus u-umlaut).
  - ʀ-umlaut: runic East Norse *glaʀ* vs. West Norse *gler* 'glass'.
  — [Wikipedia: Old Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse)
- **Old Norse strong (ablaut) verb classes**, with infinitive > preterite:
  1. *rísa* > *reis*
  2. *bjóða* > *bauð*
  3. *brenna* > *brann*
  4. *bera* > *bar*
  5. *gefa* > *gaf*
  6. *fara* > *fór*
  7. *gráta* > *grét*
  — [Wikipedia: Old Norse morphology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse_morphology)
- **Old Norse weak verbs** form the past with a dental suffix (-t, -d, -ð):
  - 1st conjugation has i-umlaut from an old *-j-* suffix: *spyrja* / pret. *spurða*; *dœma* / *dœmda*.
  - The causative *brœnna* is i-umlauted from *brenna*.
  - Present-preterites (*á, kann, skal, mun, veit*) use strong-verb ablaut in the present and weak forms in the past.
  - Some *-ra* verbs (*snúa, slá*) are strong verbs with weak preterites, a typical analogy-produced hybrid.
  — [Wikipedia: Old Norse morphology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse_morphology)
- **Old Norse syncope creating stem alternation:** *hamarr* 'hammer' > plural *hamrar*, dative plural *hǫmrum* (syncope plus u-umlaut). — [Wikipedia: Old Norse morphology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse_morphology)
- **Grammaticalization cline** (Hopper & Traugott): content word > grammatical word > clitic > inflectional affix. Its mechanisms are semantic bleaching, decategorialization, phonetic erosion and obligatorification. Hopper's principles are layering, divergence, specialization, persistence and decategorialization. — [Wikipedia: Grammaticalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammaticalization)
- **Grammaticalization examples:**
  - Latin *cantare habeo* ('I have to sing') became the Romance future, replacing the original forms.
  - Old English *willan* 'want' became the auxiliary *will*, then the clitic *'ll*.
  - *going to* > *gonna*.
  - *-ly* < Proto-Germanic *\*līką* 'body'.
  - Italian/Spanish *-mente* from 'mind'.
  - *let us* > *let's*.
  - Grammaticalization is largely unidirectional, with rare degrammaticalization (North Saami abessive *-haga* > postposition > adverb).
  — [Wikipedia: Grammaticalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammaticalization)
- **Norse-specific grammaticalization:**
  - The reflexive pronoun *sik* contracted to *-k/-sk/-zk* and became the mediopassive suffix (*kalla-sk*, *læzk* < *láta*), with later forms *-z, -s, -zt, -zst*.
  - The definite article became a suffix: *hundr-inn* 'the hound', *eyra-t* 'the ear', with forms *-inn, -in, -it, -a, -nar, -num*.
  — [Wikipedia: Old Norse morphology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse_morphology)
- **Possible source conflict:** the main Old Norse article (as summarized) says the article "did not attach to the noun until later" and declined independently (*troll / trollit*). The morphology article presents it as already suffixed. This may be a summarization artifact (both parts of noun + article inflect). — [Wikipedia: Old Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse) vs. [Wikipedia: Old Norse morphology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse_morphology)
- Frequent words are more susceptible to sound change, and grammar can block a change (Brazilian Portuguese infinitive /r/). Both are levers for principled exceptions. — [Wikibooks: Common sound changes](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Conlang/Intermediate/History/Common_sound_changes)

### Inferences
- **Recipe for intentional irregularity in Norsund:**
  1. Give Old Norsund regular suffixes that *trigger* umlaut: a plural *-i*, a causative *-ja-*, a dative *-u*.
  2. Apply umlaut, then delete the trigger by apocope or syncope.
  3. You now have umlaut plurals (cf. *\*manniz* > *menn*) and umlaut causatives (cf. *brenna* > *brœnna*).
  4. In a later epoch, apply analogical leveling to *most* nouns (they take a productive regular plural) but exempt a short list of high-frequency words: man, foot, tooth, kin terms, 'star', 'boat', 'oar'. That list is where irregulars survive in real languages.
- Make the ablaut classes *pre-proto*: give Old Norsund 4–7 vowel-gradation patterns for verbs, then let sound changes blur them (as Old Norse *bera/bar* vs. *gefa/gaf* show near-merger of classes 4 and 5). Later, analogy can move a few strong verbs into the weak (dental) class, with hybrid forms like the Norse *-ra* verbs left in between.
- **Grammaticalize the definite article and a mediopassive natively:**
  - A demonstrative *\*hinn*-like word cliticizes to give a suffixed article.
  - A reflexive *\*sik*-like pronoun gives a *-sk*-like mediopassive.
  - This creates Scandinavian *structure* without copying Norse words.
  - Kedre, by contrast, could grammaticalize a 'have'-periphrasis into a synthetic future (the *cantare habeo* path). The two languages would then have recognizably different grammaticalization histories.
- **Keep a "change log" per irregular word** (proto-form > each stage > leveled or not, and why: frequency, register, taboo). This keeps irregularity a documented design decision, not noise.

### Gaps
- No conlanger-authored source (Peterson, Biblaridion, Rosenfelder) on "intentional irregularity" was retrieved. The recipe above is inferred from natural-language mechanisms.
- Kuryłowicz's and Mańczak's laws of analogy (which forms analogy tends to target) were not covered in the fetched page.
- Familiar English examples (*help/holp* > *helped*, *kine* > *cows*, *brethren/brothers* divergence) are standard textbook cases but were not found in fetched sources.

## Q3. Which features give Old Norse, Icelandic and the Scandinavian languages their recognizable feel, and which should be used lightly to avoid a cipher of Norse?

### Takeaway
The Norse "feel" comes from a cluster of features:
- Front rounded and umlauted vowels written with special letters (æ ø œ ǫ y, plus á-type length marks).
- þ/ð.
- Consonant-heavy closed syllables and geminates.
- A nominative *-r*.
- Initial clusters with *j* from breaking (*skj-, gj-, hj-*).
- A suffixed definite article.
- Ablaut verbs.
- Patronymic *-son/-dóttir*.
- Kenning and alliteration poetics.

Using *every* marker, especially Norse diacritics plus actual Norse roots, reads as cosplay. Using the *processes* (umlaut, breaking, rhotacism, initial j/w loss) on invented roots yields the flavor without the cipher.

### Cited Findings
- **Vowels and orthography:** Old Norse had short/long vowel pairs, with length marked by an acute accent (á é í ó ú ý) in standard orthography. The normalized spelling uses æ, ø, œ, ǫ (o-ogonek). 19th-century normalization separates þ (voiceless) and ð (voiced), though early manuscripts used þ for both and the contrast was not phonemic. — [Wikipedia: Old Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse)
- **i-, u- and ʀ-umlaut** (*menn* < *\*manniz*; *lǫnd* < *\*landu*; *gler* 'glass') are core Norse processes. — [Wikipedia: Old Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse)
- **Breaking (fracture):** /e/ > *ja/jǫ* before a back vowel (*gjalda* < *\*geldaną*; *skjǫldr* < *\*skelduz*). West Norse broke only /e/; East Norse also broke /i/; a preceding /w, l, ʀ/ blocked it. This is the source of the very Norse *sk-j-, g-j-, h-j-* onsets. — [Wikipedia: Old Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse)
- **Rhotacism and nominative -r:** Proto-Germanic *-z* > ʀ > *r* (*\*wulfaz* > *ulfr* 'wolf'). After a long vowel or stem-final *l/n/s* the *-r* assimilated (*Óðinn* < *Óðinʀ*); after a short vowel it was dropped (*jarl*). Masculine a-stems take *-r* (*armr* 'arm', *hamarr* 'hammer'); feminine ō-stems do not (*gjǫf*). — [Wikipedia: Old Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse); [Wikipedia: Old Norse morphology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse_morphology)
- **Loss of initial j-, v-/w-** before certain vowels: Old Norse *orð, úlfr, ár* vs. English *word, wolf, year*. — [Wikipedia: Old Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse)
- **Holtzmann's law:** Proto-Germanic *\*jj, \*ww* > *ggj, ggv*. — [Wikipedia: Old Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse)
- **Weak nouns** end in *-i* (masc., *bogi*) and *-a* (fem., *varta*). — [Wikipedia: Old Norse morphology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse_morphology)
- **Suffixed definite article** (*hundr-inn*), **mediopassive -sk** (*kallask*) and **strong verbs** (*bera/bar, fara/fór*): see Q2. — [Wikipedia: Old Norse morphology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse_morphology)
- **Built-in dialect contrasts:**
  - West Norse kept diphthongs (*ei, au*) and had more u-umlaut. East Norse monophthongized them (merging with /eː/, /øː/) and had less u-umlaut.
  - Modern Icelandic *lönd* vs. Swedish *land* (neuter plural).
  - Epenthesis in *aptr* varied by region: West Norwegian *aftur*, north of Bergen *aftir*, East Norwegian *aftær*.
  - Nasal vowels merged into oral vowels in most of East Norse around the 11th century but survived in Dalecarlian.
  — [Wikipedia: Old Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse)
- **Patronymics:** the father's name in the genitive (usually *-s*, or *-i* > *-a*) + *-son* / *-dóttir* (Jón Einarsson's children are Ólafur Jónsson and Sigríður Jónsdóttir). Matronymics use the mother's genitive (*-ar*, or *-a* > *-u*): Ólafur Bryndísarson. Since 2019 Iceland allows *-bur* ("child of") for registered nonbinary people. Iceland kept the old pan-Northern-European system; Denmark, Norway, Sweden and the Faroes re-allowed patronymics in 2005, 2002, 1982 and 1992 respectively. — [Wikipedia: Icelandic name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icelandic_name)
- **Kennings:** "a figuratively phrased compound term" with a base-word plus a determinant:
  - 'whale's road' (*hron rade*) = sea.
  - *gjálfr-marr* 'sea-steed' = ship; *báru fákr* 'wave's horse' = ship.
  - *íss rauðra randa* 'icicle of red shields' = sword.
  - *grand viðar* 'bane of wood' = fire.
  Related devices:
  - **Heiti** are single-word poetic synonyms (prose *hestr* 'horse' vs. poetic *fákr, marr*).
  - Kennings can nest (*tvíkent*). Snorri's *Skáldskaparmál* accepts up to five elements and condemns mixed metaphor (*nykrat*).
  - Kennings are strongly tied to alliterative verse, including skaldic *dróttkvætt*.
  — [Wikipedia: Kenning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenning)
- **Place-name elements** (as preserved in Normandy, and useful as a "Norse naming kit"): *bekkr* 'brook', *búð* 'booth', *brekka* 'slope', *kjarr* 'marsh', *dalr* 'valley', *skáli* 'shelter', *steinn* 'stone', *garðr* 'enclosure', *haugr* 'mound', *holmr* 'islet', *hús* 'house', *topt* 'house-site', *torp* 'settlement', *þveit* 'clearing', *vík* 'bay', *klif* 'cliff'. — [Wikipedia: Norman toponymy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_toponymy)
- Norse words in English include *egg, knife, sky, window*; Northern Middle English (Danelaw) and Early Scots were strongly affected. — [Wikipedia: Old Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse)
- Rosenfelder notes that some conlangs "aim to duplicate the feel of a particular language, or language family." He made Verdurian "look mildly familiar, as if it could be a distant relative of the European languages" by borrowing from several real languages. — [Zompist: Language Construction Kit](https://www.zompist.com/kitlong.html)
- Peterson (per interview summaries) says the main way to make conlangs sound distinct is changing *phonotactics*, "the rules of how sounds are put together," along with intonation and stress. — [Little White Lies, search snippet](https://lwlies.com/articles/how-do-you-create-a-real-fake-language/)

### Inferences
- **"Use heavily" features.** These are structural, and readers feel them without recognizing a specific language:
  - Closed, consonant-final syllables and geminates.
  - Umlaut vowel alternations in plurals and causatives.
  - Initial *j*-glide clusters from breaking (*skj-, hj-, gj-, bj-*).
  - A nominative *-r* that assimilates after *l/n* (giving *-ll, -nn* finals).
  - A suffixed article.
  - Ablaut verbs.
  - Patronymic surnames.
  - Kenning-rich poetic register.
- **"Use lightly" features.** These are recognition triggers that become a cipher if overused:
  - Too many diacritics or special letters at once (æ, ø, ǫ, þ, ð and acute length all together reads as normalized Old Norse). Pick two or three: e.g., þ/ð plus ø, *or* å-style plus æ.
  - Real Norse roots in names (*-heim, Ragn-, -ulf*). If used at all, deform them through Norsund's own sound changes so the root is perceivable but not identical.
  - Real god names and *Valhalla*-type terms.
- **Orthography as a flavor dial.** A Norsund spelling with þ/ð/æ signals "old, Icelandic-like." A reform-era spelling (e.g., *th/dh*, *ae*, *aa*) signals a later scribal tradition, perhaps Kedre-influenced. The orthography itself can be a historical layer: the island scribes' runes or letters vs. Kedre-script spellings.
- **West/East Norse is a ready template for Norsund sibling dialects.** One dialect keeps diphthongs and u-umlaut (conservative, insular, Icelandic-like); another monophthongizes and loses u-umlaut (mainland, East-Norse-like). Epenthetic-vowel color (*-ur / -ir / -ær*) can be a shibboleth that varies by island.

### Gaps
- No fetched source documents *-heim* as a place-name element or describes the Danish/Norwegian/Swedish modern feel: Danish stød, Swedish/Norwegian pitch accent, the *kj-/sj-* fricatives of modern Swedish/Norwegian, *å* orthography, consonant gradation such as Danish soft d. These are standard knowledge but unverified in this session.
- Assimilations like *nk > kk* (*drekka* 'drink') and *nt > tt* were not covered in the fetched Old Norse page.
- No source was found on reader or audience perception studies of "what sounds Norse."

## Q4. What gives Italian and Venetian their feel, how could such a layer appear as loans in a Norse-like language, and what real Norse–Romance contact examples exist?

### Takeaway
Standard Italian's signature is contrastive geminates, syntactic doubling, open syllables with final *-o/-a/-e/-i*, penultimate stress and affricates. Venetian differs in the opposite direction on several points, which suits a "Viking Venice":
- It has *no geminates*.
- It drops final vowels after continuants (*pien, altar*).
- It weakens intervocalic *l* to the vanishing "ł" (*góndoła* > *góndoa*).
- It velarizes final *-n* [ŋ].
- It writes /z/ as ⟨x⟩ (*xe*, medieval *caxa, raxon*).

The real-world contact analogues are a mirror image of the Norsund case, and their mechanics transfer directly:
- In **Normandy**, Norse gave about 150 words and many place-name elements to Romance.
- In **Sicily**, Norman/Old French, Occitan, Greek and Arabic loan *layers* coexist.
- In **post-1066 English**, a Germanic language absorbed a Romance conquerors' layer.

### Cited Findings

**Italian phonology**
- Geminates are contrastive (*fato/fatto, note/notte, sete/sette, casa/cassa, caro/carro, papa/pappa*). Gemination shortens the preceding vowel. /ɲ ʃ ʎ dz ts/ are always geminate between vowels.
- Raddoppiamento sintattico doubles a word-initial consonant after certain words or a stressed final vowel: *a casa* [akˈkaːza]; *parlò portoghese* [parˈlɔpportoˈɡeːze]. It is standard in central/southern Italy and inconsistent in the north.
- Stress is lexical, with the penult preferred.
- There are seven stressed vowels /i e ɛ a ɔ o u/, neutralized to close-mid when unstressed.
- Affricates are /ts dz tʃ dʒ/.
— [Wikipedia: Italian phonology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_phonology)
- The fetched article does not explicitly state that "most native words end in vowels." It says codas are restricted. — [Wikipedia: Italian phonology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_phonology)

**Venetian phonology and orthography**
- **Evanescent L (ł):** *góndoła* can be [ˈgondoea], [ˈgondola] or [ˈgondoa]. Where ł vanishes, *scóła* 'school' and *scóa* 'broom' merge as [ˈskoa]. Some orthographies write ⟨ł⟩, but Venetian was "never traditionally written with it." — [Wikipedia: Venetian language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venetian_language)
- **Apocope:** the masculine singular ending is often unpronounced after continuants, especially in rural speech (Italian *pieno* > *pien*, *altare* > *altar*). Central-southern varieties delete only after /n/; northern ones also after dental stops and velars. — [Wikipedia: Venetian language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venetian_language)
- **No geminates:** Italian *fette, palla, penna* = Venetian *féte, bała, péna*. — [Wikipedia: Venetian language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venetian_language)
- **Velar nasal:** final /n/ is velarized (*man* [ˈmaŋ] 'hand'). — [Wikipedia: Venetian language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venetian_language)
- **Interdentals:** some dialects have [θ], which is socially stigmatized and often replaced by [s] or [ts]. Old [ð] shifted to [dz], [z] or [d] by dialect. This is a ready-made rural vs. urban prestige marker. — [Wikipedia: Venetian language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venetian_language)
- **⟨x⟩ = /z/:** medieval *raxon* 'reason', *caxa* 'house'; modern initial *xe* 'is'. Between vowels ⟨s⟩ = /z/ and ⟨ss⟩ = /s/ (*basa* 'kisses' vs. *bassa* 'low'). ⟨ç⟩ and ⟨z⟩ were inconsistent in old texts. /ʃ/ is written *s-c(i)* (*s-ciao*). Grave and acute accents mark stress and vowel quality. — [Wikipedia: Venetian language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venetian_language)
- **Lexical layers:**
  - *Ciao* < Venetian *s-ciao* 'slave' < Medieval Latin *sclavus*.
  - Greek loans: *apotèca* 'pharmacy', *masa* 'too much'.
  - Arabic loan: *bagígi* 'peanuts'.
  - Germanic/Gothic and French items: *trincàr* 'drink', French-derived *matelot*.
  — [Wikipedia: Venetian language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venetian_language)

**Norse into Romance: Normandy**
- Norman inherited "some 150 words from Old Norse" (Ridel 2010). Maritime and coastal examples:
  - *mauve* 'gull' < *mávar*
  - *hommet* 'islet' < *hólmr*
  - *nez* 'headland' < *nes*
  - *mielle* 'dune' < *melr*
  - *milgreu* 'beach grass' < *\*melgrös*
  - *hougue* 'mound' < *haugr*
  - *viquet* < *vík*, the source of French *guichet* and English *wicket*
  - *génotte* 'peanut' < *\*jarðhnot*
  - *griller* 'slide' < *\*skriðla*
  Retention of aspirated /h/ in Norman is sometimes, disputedly, attributed to Norse. — [Wikipedia: Norman language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_language)
- **Toponymy:** Norse elements nativized into Romance spelling and phonology:
  - *bekkr* > *-bec* (Caudebec), *búð* > *-beuf* (Elbeuf, Criquebeuf), *topt* > *-tot* (Yvetot).
  - *dalr* > *Dalle-/-dal* (Dieppedalle), *haugr* > *Hougue*, *holmr* > *Houlme*, *steinn* > *Étain-*, *garðr* > *-gard*, *þveit* > *-thuit/-tuit*, *vík* > *-vic/-vy*, *klif* > *-clives*.
  - *-fleur* is Old English, not Norse.
  — [Wikipedia: Norman toponymy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_toponymy)
- **Adaptation patterns:**
  - Norse *Ketill* > Quetteville, Cretteville.
  - *Sigfríðr* > Chiffre-/Cheffre-.
  - *Blakkr* > Blacque-.
  - [a] > [e] before [r]: *Barki* > Berquetot.
  - Later names follow Romance head-first order (Martintot).
  - Anglo-Scandinavian hybrids appear (Quittebeuf = OE *Hwita* + Norse *búð*; Auppegard = OE *æppel* + Norse *garðr*).
  — [Wikipedia: Norman toponymy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_toponymy)

**Romance conquerors over Germanic: England, and Sicily's layers**
- Norman French entered English after the Norman conquest (Anglo-Norman), e.g., *castle, garden, poor*. — [Wikipedia: Norman language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_language)
- **Sicily (Norman conquest from 1061).** Loans from Norman/Old French:
  - *accattari* 'buy' < Norman *acater*
  - *custureri* 'tailor' < *cousturier*
  - *bucceri* 'butcher' < *bouchier*
  - *racina* 'grape' < *raisin*
  - *raggia* 'anger' < *rage*
  - *foddi* 'mad' < *fol*
  - *giugnettu* 'July' < *juignet*
  A second wave of Parisian French came under Charles I of Anjou (13th c.). — [Wikipedia: Sicilian language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicilian_language)
- **Sicilian stratification:**
  - Greek (Byzantine from 535): *carusu* 'boy' < *koûros*, *cona* 'icon'.
  - Arabic (9th–10th c.): about 300 words, mostly agriculture and irrigation, e.g., *zàgara* 'blossom' < *zahra*, *gèbbia* 'irrigation pond', *ràisi* 'leader' < *raʾīs*.
  - Occitan, via the court and the Sicilian School of poetry: *burgisi* 'landowner', *pariggiu* 'equal'.
  - Gallo-Italic colonies.
  - "Twins" is attested from multiple layers at once: *giameddi* (Italian), *bizzuni* (Old French/Catalan), *binelli* (Ligurian), *èmmuli* (Latin), *ièmiddi* (Greek).
  — [Wikipedia: Sicilian language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicilian_language)
- **Peterson's Ghiscari-Valyrian model** is the fictional analogue: the conquerors' grammar won, and the conquered language survived in vocabulary and pronunciation. — [dothraki.com: The State of Valyrian](https://dothraki.com/2014/05/the-state-of-valyrian/)

### Inferences
- **Which way the contact runs.** In Norsund's setting the Norse-like language *survives* and absorbs a Latinate conquerors' layer. That is the **English-after-1066** pattern, not the Normandy pattern (where Romance won). Model Kedre as a *superstrate*: an elite or administrative language that leaves vocabulary in government, law, church/astronomy, cuisine and fashion, while Norsund keeps its core grammar and basic vocabulary.
- **Three Kedre loan strata for Norsund**, mirroring the Sicilian "twins" example:
  - **(a) Early/popular loans**, taken before or during the conquest. They undergo *all* later Norsund sound changes: final vowels lost, umlaut where triggered, nominative *-r* added. They end up looking native. Example pattern: a Kedre-like *\*kastellu* > Norsund *kastell*, plural with umlaut.
  - **(b) Conquest-era administrative loans**. Partially adapted; Kedre's final *-e/-o* is often clipped to fit Norsund's closed syllables (cf. Venetian's own apocope *pieno* > *pien*).
  - **(c) Late learned loans** from written Kedre, taken by scholars and star-priests. Little adapted; they keep vowel-final endings and Kedre spellings.
  The same Kedre root borrowed twice gives native/learned *doublets* (one worn down, one pristine) that can split in meaning.
- **Loan adaptation rules** modeled on Norman toponymy, but reversed:
  - Kedre voiced intervocalic consonants may surface as Norsund fricatives (ð/ɣ).
  - Kedre *ts/tʃ* may map to *s/sk* or *k*, the way Norse *Ketill* was respelled *Quette-* in Romance.
  - Kedre stress (penultimate) may shift to Norsund's initial stress, which then reduces the unstressed final vowels.
  This adaptation gives each loan a visible "accent."
- **The prestige city dialect as a Venetian-styled Norsund.** Apply Venetian-like changes to *Norsund* itself, not Italian vocabulary:
  - Lenition or loss of an intervocalic liquid (a "ł" effect: *\*-ala-* > *-aa-*).
  - Degemination: Norse geminates simplified (*-ll-* > *-l-*), which sounds softer and more "southern."
  - Final -n > [ŋ].
  - Stigmatizing an old [θ] as rustic, so the city says [s] or [ts] where islanders keep þ. This mirrors Venetian [θ] being stigmatized.
  - Spelling /z/ with ⟨x⟩ in the city's orthographic tradition as a visual signature (e.g., city *Xel-* vs. rural *Sel-*).
  Together these deliver a "Venice" flavor from regular dialect sound change, plus a Kedre-heavy loan vocabulary for the star cult, canals and trade.
- **Bridging features.** Geminates exist in both Old Norse (*hamarr, menn*) and Italian (*fatto*), so they can be the overlap that makes the blend feel coherent. Italian-style penultimate stress and vowel-final words are the *Kedre* markers. Venetian-style apocope and degemination make the city speech sound distinctly *not* Kedre.
- **Lexical domains.** Real contact layers cluster by domain (Arabic in Sicilian agriculture and irrigation; Norse in Norman maritime and coastal words). So Kedre loans should cluster in imperial administration, law, liturgy/astronomy and luxury goods. Norsund-native words should dominate the sea, boats, weather, kinship and the body. The canal city may also *export* Norsund maritime words back into Kedre, as Norse *vík* became French *guichet* and English *wicket*.
- **Real naming kit for canal-city toponyms:** Norse elements with Romance phonology (*-bec, -tot, -gard, -houlme, -vic*). For the reverse (Norsund names for Kedre places), use Norse heads with clipped Latinate determinants.

### Gaps
- No fetched source covers Venetian's full intervocalic lenition and loss of Latin consonants (e.g., Latin *-t-* > *-d-* > ∅ in Venetian forms). Only ł and the [ð] outcomes are sourced.
- The often-cited Venetian contributions to Italian and English (*ghetto, arsenale, lazaretto, gondola*) were not verified in the fetched page (the article summary did not mention them).
- No source was found on *direct* Norse–Italian contact (e.g., Varangians in Byzantium and Italy, runic inscriptions on the Piraeus Lion in Venice). Worth a targeted search if a historical precedent for "Vikings in Venice" is wanted.
- Siculo-Norman: the fetched page shows Norman *French* (a Romance language with a Norse substrate) influencing Sicilian, not Norse itself. No Norse words in Sicilian were found.

## Q5. How do conlangers model dialects and sociolects (prestige city vs. rural), archaic/liturgical registers, honorifics, and superstrate/substrate and diglossia layers?

### Takeaway
The best-documented models come from real sociolinguistics plus Peterson's Valyrian setup:
- Dialects are the *same proto-forms run through partly different rule sets*. Isolation gives conservatism (Dragonstone); size, contact and a large lower class give divergence (Meereen).
- Diglossia (Ferguson) supplies the template for a sacred or archaic H register beside a vernacular L. Fishman extends it to *unrelated* languages, so Kedre can be H over Norsund L.
- Substrate languages leave vocabulary and accent; superstrates leave prestige vocabulary.
- No fetched source covered honorific design in conlangs.

### Cited Findings
- **Peterson's dialect model:**
  - Astapori, Yunkish and Meereenese are dialects of one language with similar grammars and different sounds.
  - Yunkish was left nearly identical to Astapori (economy of effort).
  - Meereen diverged most because it is the biggest city with the largest lower class.
  - Dragonstone stayed conservative through isolation; only Common Tongue pronunciation affected it ("This is why older pronunciations of j and v aren't maintained").
  - Rate of change rises with contact and with distance from the homeland.
  - It takes about three generations for a language to disappear from a family, and five or six to stamp out Ghiscari if the rulers wanted to.
  — [dothraki.com: The State of Valyrian](https://dothraki.com/2014/05/the-state-of-valyrian/)
- **Where change comes from:** in research for *Advanced Language Construction*, Rosenfelder found that all the ongoing sound changes he identified were "divergences from the standard, rather than imitations of society's leaders." Change tends to come from below, not top-down from prestige. — [Zompist blog, June 2012, search snippet](https://zompist.wordpress.com/2012/06/)
- **Areal diffusion:** neighboring languages share changes across family lines (uvular R in French and German). — [Ask Zompist](https://zompist.wordpress.com/2019/01/26/ask-zompist-common-sound-changes/)
- **Ferguson's diglossia (1959):** a "relatively stable language situation" with a primary vernacular (L) and a "very divergent, highly codified" superposed variety (H). H carries a respected literature, is learned in school, and is not used for ordinary conversation.
  - H domains: writing, literature, education, sermons, news, government speeches.
  - L domains: home, friends, markets.
  - H is high-prestige and standardized; L is acquired natively and is often unwritten.
  - H vocabulary and forms "filter down" into the vernacular in altered form.
  - Examples: MSA vs. Arabic vernaculars; Standard vs. Swiss German; Katharevousa (purist H) vs. Dimotiki (Dimotiki sole standard from 1976); French vs. Haitian Creole; Classical vs. Vulgar Latin; Middle Egyptian vs. later Egyptian; literary vs. colloquial Tamil.
  — [Wikipedia: Diglossia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diglossia)
- **Fishman's extension:** diglossia can involve unrelated languages (Alsace: French H, Alsatian L). Kloss's terms are exoglossia and endoglossia. — [Wikipedia: Diglossia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diglossia)
- **Socially marked pronunciations:** Venetian [θ] is stigmatized and replaced by [s]/[ts], and apocope is stronger in rural varieties. Real examples of an urban/rural or prestige axis expressed in phonology. — [Wikipedia: Venetian language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venetian_language)
- **Poetic/archaic register devices:** heiti (poetic synonyms distinct from prose words: *fákr* vs. *hestr* 'horse') and kennings. A register with its own lexicon is attested in Norse. — [Wikipedia: Kenning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenning)
- **Contact outcomes by social stratum:** a conquering elite's language can become the grammatical base while the conquered language survives in vocabulary, especially among people never taught the elite language. — [dothraki.com: The State of Valyrian](https://dothraki.com/2014/05/the-state-of-valyrian/)
- **Layered borrowing** from successive prestige languages (Greek, then Arabic, then Norman French, then Occitan, then Italian) is attested in Sicilian, with domain clustering (Arabic agriculture). — [Wikipedia: Sicilian language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicilian_language)

### Inferences
- **A three-tier register system for the setting:**
  - **(H1) Liturgical/astronomical Kedre**, or an archaic "Old Kedre": the star cult's sacred language, learned only in temples and schools.
  - **(H2) Old Norsund**: the settlers' saga and law register, kept in oaths, skaldic verse and the founding chronicles. It is archaic but native, like Katharevousa purism. It uses heiti and kennings and keeps distinctions (u-umlaut, þ, nominative *-r*) that the vernacular lost.
  - **(L) Modern Norsund vernaculars**: rural/island dialects plus the canal-city dialect.
  This creates Fishman-style diglossia (Kedre over Norsund) *and* Ferguson-style diglossia (Old over Modern Norsund) at once.
- **City dialect as prestige vs. innovation.** Following Rosenfelder (change from below) and Peterson (the biggest city diverges most), the canal city's *spoken* dialect can be phonologically innovative (degemination, ł-loss, [θ] > [s]) while its *written* standard is archaizing. Rural islanders then sound "older" in phonology (they keep þ and geminates) but are stigmatized socially. That is the Venetian [θ] pattern exactly.
- **Star-obsession lexicon as a register.** Astronomical terms can exist in all three tiers: a native kenning ("sky-nail," "night-sail"), an Old Norsund heiti, and a learned Kedre loan. Speakers would choose among them by register, like Sicilian's multiple words for 'twins'.
- **Dialect economy.** Peterson made Yunkish nearly identical to Astapori. Fully derive only the languages the story needs on-page (the city dialect and one rural dialect); define the others as a few shibboleth differences.
- **Honorifics (design suggestion, unsourced).** A Kedre-derived honorific or formal pronoun layer used in the city (court and clergy), vs. Norsund kin terms and patronymic address in the countryside, would follow the general superstrate-into-politeness pattern. This needs sourcing before being stated as a real-world generalization.

### Gaps
- No fetched source covers how conlangers design honorific systems or T-V pronoun distinctions. Peterson's and Rosenfelder's treatments of politeness were not retrieved. The LCK long page fetched did not contain register or politeness sections.
- No fetched source documents a *liturgical* language case specifically (Church Slavonic, Latin in the Catholic church, Sanskrit, Ge'ez). Diglossia covers Classical vs. Vulgar Latin only in passing.
- Formal definitions of substrate/superstrate/adstrate (e.g., Wikipedia "Stratum (linguistics)") were not fetched. The terms are used here in their standard sense, backed only by the Valyrian, Norman and Sicilian examples.

## Q6. Which tools apply sound changes (Zompist SCA², Lexurgy, others), and how are they used?

### Takeaway
SCA² (Rosenfelder, 2012) is the classic browser tool. You define categories, write ordered X/Y/environment rules, paste a lexicon, and inspect intermediate stages. Lexurgy (Kotlin, GPL-3.0, by GitHub user def-gthill) is a newer applier with a web app and CLI. Index Diachronica is the companion reference for finding attested changes. Plain SCAs are known to handle syllable-based changes (syncope) and long-distance umlaut poorly, which matters for a Norse-like language that depends on both.

### Cited Findings
- **SCA²** (Mark Rosenfelder, 2012, JavaScript) takes four inputs:
  - **Categories:** `NAME=sounds`, e.g., `V=aeiou`, `L=āēīōū`, `S=ptc`, `Z=bdg`.
  - **Ordered sound changes**, each applied to the output of the previous one.
  - **An input lexicon.**
  - **Separate rewrite rules** (e.g., `lh|lj`).
  — [Zompist: SCA²](https://www.zompist.com/sca2.html)
- **SCA² rule syntax** is `X/Y/environment`:
  - `i/j/_V` (i > j before a vowel)
  - `S/Z/V_V` (category-to-category intervocalic voicing, a lenition)
  - Empty replacement deletes: `p//V_t`, `e//C_rV`
  - `#` is a word boundary: `[sm]//_#` deletes final s or m
  The demo lexicon is Latin (*lector, doctor, focus, cīvitatem, adoptare*) turned into Romance-like forms (e.g., `gn/nh/_`). — [Zompist: SCA²](https://www.zompist.com/sca2.html)
- **SCA² output options:** output only, input > output, or output [input]. It can show differences from the last run, report which rules applied, show intermediate results, and download the output. — [Zompist: SCA²](https://www.zompist.com/sca2.html)
- **Lexurgy** is a sound-change applier that "simulates historical sound changes" by applying regular rules to a word list. It is written in Kotlin with an ANTLR grammar for its file format and licensed GPL-3.0, owned by GitHub user def-gthill. It runs as a CLI (Java 8+) and a web app at lexurgy.com/sc. Docs are listed at meamoria.com/lexurgy/html/index.html (which returned 404 when fetched). — [GitHub: def-gthill/lexurgy](https://github.com/def-gthill/lexurgy)
- **SCA limitations:** they "struggle with syllable-specific changes like syncope and multi-syllable environments like umlaut." — [Wikibooks: Common sound changes](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Conlang/Intermediate/History/Common_sound_changes)
- **Index Diachronica search** works by IPA segment, diacritic or feature class ([+labial], [-voice], [+nasal]), letting you look up what a sound has become in real languages. — [Index Diachronica (chridd)](https://chridd.nfshost.com/diachronica/)
- Eldamo writes each Tolkien change in SCA-like notation, e.g., `[{ăŏŭ}{C|CC}i] > [{eœy}{C|CC}i]` for i-fronting and `[-VCi] > [-ViC]` for i-intrusion. Tolkien's histories map directly onto applier rules. — [Eldamo i-fronting](https://eldamo.org/content/words/word-3958031275.html); [Eldamo i-intrusion](https://eldamo.org/content/words/word-2399289739.html)

### Inferences
- **Practical workflow for Norsund:**
  1. Write the Old Norsund lexicon (a few hundred roots plus inflected forms that carry umlaut triggers).
  2. Encode each epoch as an SCA² or Lexurgy rule block.
  3. Use "show intermediate results" to snapshot Early Norsund, Contact Norsund and Modern Norsund.
  4. Fork the final block into a city-dialect file and a rural-dialect file.
  5. Run Kedre loans as a *separate input list* injected at the contact stage, so they undergo only the later rules. This mechanically produces the early-loan vs. learned-loan contrast.
- **Umlaut in SCA².** SCAs handle umlaut poorly, so implement it in two steps: (i) mark the vowel with a temporary symbol when an *i/j* follows within the word (`a/ä/_C(C)i`); (ii) delete the trigger with apocope or syncope rules afterward. Alternatively, use Lexurgy, which, as a more modern applier, may handle syllables and features better. This is unverified because the docs could not be fetched.
- **Version-control the rule files** in the lore repo alongside the lexicon, so each derived word is reproducible and any later rule change regenerates the whole lexicon consistently.
