## 1. Production case studies: how professional conlangs were built, scoped and delivered

### Takeaway
Professional screen and game conlangs nearly always start from a small attested corpus. That corpus can be a few scripted lines, about 50 book words, or a dozen names. The language is reverse-engineered so the old material fits. The contractor delivers a grammar, a lexicon scoped to the script, written translations with phonetic respellings, and audio at several speeds. Drift on set is normal: actors, showrunners, editors and other designers change the language after delivery. Games sometimes deliberately build only a shallow "smoke and mirrors" language tied to a mechanic.

### Cited Findings

**Klingon (Marc Okrand, Paramount, from 1984)**
- Okrand was hired to develop Klingon for *Star Trek III: The Search for Spock* (1984). His starting point was the handful of Klingon lines already spoken in *Star Trek: The Motion Picture* (1979). He used those lines as the base for the full language — [StarTrek.com interview pt 1 [snippet]](https://startrek.com/news/qapla-klingon-language-creator-marc-okrand-part-1); [SlashFilm [snippet]](https://www.slashfilm.com/1739817/star-trek-klingon-language-creator-marc-okrand)
- Okrand and producer Harve Bennett agreed the language "had to be real." So Okrand built a real phonology and grammar instead of inventing random sounds. He wrote *The Klingon Dictionary* expecting only casual interest and did not foresee people becoming fluent — [StarTrek.com interview pt 2 [fetched]](https://www.startrek.com/news/qapla-klingon-language-creator-marc-okrand-part-2)
- Okrand was not involved in TNG's first season. He joined in season 2, after staff needed Klingon lines that were not in the dictionary. He never worked directly with TNG actors, and TNG staff wrote most of the later Klingon, some of it following the dictionary closely and some not. For DS9 and Voyager, writers created the dialogue — [StarTrek.com pt 2 [fetched]](https://www.startrek.com/news/qapla-klingon-language-creator-marc-okrand-part-2)
- For *Star Trek* (2009), Okrand worked only from script excerpts, not the full script. He delivered transcripts and MP3 recordings to the actors and the dialogue coach, and visited the set only once — [StarTrek.com pt 2 [fetched]](https://www.startrek.com/news/qapla-klingon-language-creator-marc-okrand-part-2)
- Klingon romanization uses uppercase letters to mark sounds that do not exist in English, which tells actors to take special care with them. Okrand said Christopher Lloyd (Kruge) was "a natural" — [CNN [snippet]](https://amp.cnn.com/cnn/style/article/star-trek-klingon-marc-okrand); [SlashFilm [snippet]](https://www.slashfilm.com/1739817/star-trek-klingon-language-creator-marc-okrand)

**Dothraki and High Valyrian (David J. Peterson, HBO, from 2009)**
- In 2009 HBO turned to the Language Creation Society (LCS), which Peterson had co-founded in 2007, and the LCS ran a contest. Applicants had to devise a phonology and lexicon and translate dialogue from the pilot. Peterson won against about 30 to 35 proposals — [Wikipedia: David J. Peterson [snippet]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_J._Peterson); [AV Club [snippet]](https://www.avclub.com/meet-game-of-thrones-resident-linguist-ki-fin-yeni-1798269356); [Mental Floss [snippet]](https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/29594/man-who-conlanged-dothraki-hbos-game-thrones)
- The application ran in September and October 2009. By November, Peterson had a "more or less complete grammar" and a vocabulary of about 1,700 words — [Scientific American guest blog [snippet]](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/guest-blog/the-dothraki-response-to-a-call-for-science-in-a-created-language/); [AV Club [snippet]](https://www.avclub.com/meet-game-of-thrones-resident-linguist-ki-fin-yeni-1798269356)
- Source corpus: George R.R. Martin's books contain about 50 Dothraki words and phrases, according to Peterson. He combined these with Martin's descriptions of how the language sounds. Peterson said building the language took "maybe a month" — [Wikipedia / press summary [snippet]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_J._Peterson); [The World/PRI [snippet]](https://theworld.org/stories/2013/08/15/professor-explains-his-creation-dothraki-language-hbos-game-thrones-tv-series)
- Dothraki later grew to more than 3,000 words and was codified in *Living Language Dothraki* (2014) — [Wikipedia: Dothraki language [snippet]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dothraki_language)
- High Valyrian was back-fitted from even less. Martin's books gave two sentences (*Valar morghulis*, *Valar dohaeris*), five or six nouns, and character names. The two *valar* sentences became the basis of the verb conjugation system — [Wikipedia: Valyrian languages [snippet]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valyrian_languages); [Mental Floss [snippet]](https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/63023/8-fun-facts-about-valyrian-language-game-thrones)
- For *House of the Dragon*, Peterson had to expand High Valyrian vocabulary because the show had more dialogue than he expected. A Babbel article puts it at 5,000+ words, but this figure is unverified — [Wikipedia: Valyrian languages [snippet]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valyrian_languages); [Babbel [snippet]](https://www.babbel.com/en/magazine/the-invented-languages-in-game-of-thrones)
- Production workflow: producers mark the lines to translate, and Peterson returns written translations plus audio files. The audio comes at normal speed, at slow speed, and syllable by syllable — [CBS News [snippet]](https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/baltimore/news/the-game-of-thrones-language-that-1-2m-people-are-learning); [TED Ideas [snippet]](https://ideas.ted.com/?p=111825)
- An actor described the packet as "the English translation, the High Valyrian, a phonetic translation, and then also audio recordings" — [Drama Quarterly [snippet]](https://dramaquarterly.com/talking-my-language/)
- Peterson is not on set, so "mispronunciations have been inevitable." Showrunners overrode his pronunciation of *khaleesi*, actors change phrasing, and editors clip sentences in post-production — [TED Ideas [snippet]](https://ideas.ted.com/?p=111825); [Film Companion [snippet]](https://filmcompanion.in/features/hollywood-features/game-of-thrones-linguist-david-peterson-on-creating-the-dothraki-language)
- Sample deliverable: Peterson's *High Valyrian Pronunciation Guide* (4 pages, created November 2020, around HotD production). It explains every letter with an English or common-European anchor word, and adds explicit "never like..." warnings. Examples:
  - "G, g: like the 'g' in 'goat' (never like the 'g' in 'genius')"
  - "Gh: like the 'r' in French 'rouge' (never like the 'gh' in 'ghost')"
  - "Q, q: likely the most difficult sound… touching the back of the tongue to the uvula"
  - Unaspirated k/p/t are taught with a "hand in front of your face" test, and long vowels are held "twice as long."
  - It ends with a stress rule: stress usually falls on the penult; the antepenult takes it when the penult is short and the antepenult is heavy; imperatives take final stress.
  - It notes where variation is allowed. For example, *j* and *v* are "chameleons" that shift in consonant clusters, and *r* varies "depending on the original language of the speaker."
  - Source: [dedalvs.com HV Pronunciation Guide [primary PDF]](https://dedalvs.com/work/house-of-the-dragon/misc/pronunciation_guide.pdf)

**Na'vi (Paul Frommer, *Avatar*, from 2005)**
- Frommer started in 2005. For the film's conversational needs he built about 500 words, partly Polynesian-inspired, around a dozen or so words James Cameron had already coined — [Wikipedia: Na'vi language [snippet]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Na%CA%BCvi_language)
- The lexicon has since grown to between about 2,300 and over 3,000 words; sources differ (2,300+ in 2024; Kelutral 2,900+; Frommer's estimate 3,000+) — [Wikipedia [snippet]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Na%CA%BCvi_language); [Kelutral [snippet]](https://kelutral.org/references/wordbuilding)

**Dovahzul / Dragon language (Bethesda, *Skyrim*, 2011)**
- Emil Pagliarulo, then Senior Designer, created the language. Todd Howard contributed the visual concept: script that looks "carved into stone with a dragon's talon." The late concept artist Adam Adamowicz drew the glyphs — [Bethesda official article [fetched]](https://elderscrolls.bethesda.net/en-AU/news/how-bethesda-game-studios-made-skyrims-dragon-language)
- Pagliarulo: the language is "first and foremost, a gameplay device for using the shouts." Each shout uses one to three single-syllable words, and delivery changes with word count. The idea was inspired by the Weirding Module sound weapons in Lynch's *Dune* (1984) — [Bethesda [fetched]](https://elderscrolls.bethesda.net/en-AU/news/how-bethesda-game-studios-made-skyrims-dragon-language)
- The vocabulary draws on Old English, Swedish, Danish and German. Pagliarulo called it much simpler than Tolkien's Sindarin, relying on "smoke and mirrors" to feel deep without being deep. The team kept an English-to-Dragon and Dragon-to-English database — [Bethesda [fetched]](https://elderscrolls.bethesda.net/en-AU/news/how-bethesda-game-studios-made-skyrims-dragon-language)
- Failure mode: keeping the database current was a struggle, because "once we got rolling, the designers started coming up with some new words and phrases" — [Bethesda [fetched]](https://elderscrolls.bethesda.net/en-AU/news/how-bethesda-game-studios-made-skyrims-dragon-language)
- Music constraint: Pagliarulo wrote the Dragonborn theme lyrics, which needed a rhyme scheme that worked in both English and Dragon. That requirement shaped the language's rhythm — [Bethesda [fetched]](https://elderscrolls.bethesda.net/en-AU/news/how-bethesda-game-studios-made-skyrims-dragon-language)
- Post-launch extension: the language had no word for "love," so Pagliarulo later coined *Ros* for a fan's wedding cake, comparing this to Klingon — [Bethesda [fetched]](https://elderscrolls.bethesda.net/en-AU/news/how-bethesda-game-studios-made-skyrims-dragon-language)
- The script has a 34-character alphabet: 25 letters map to English (no C), plus digraph and syllable characters — [The Linguafile [snippet]](https://www.thelinguafile.com/2014/02/conlangs-skyrims-dragon-language.html?m=1); [Game Informer 2011 [snippet]](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2011/01/20/skyrim-s-dragon-shouts)
- A fan community at thuum.org extends and teaches the language — [thuum.org [snippet]](https://www.thuum.org/viewthread.php?thread=35)

**Elvish for Peter Jackson's films (David Salo)**
- Salo wrote to Jackson around 1998 after hearing of the film plans. He was contracted for *The Lord of the Rings* and later *The Hobbit* to produce Elvish, Dwarvish and other material — [Fox News/AP 2001 [snippet]](https://www.foxnews.com/story/tolkien-languages-expert-helped-rings-filmmakers); [Tolkien Gateway [snippet]](https://www.tolkiengateway.net/wiki/David_Salo)
- He translated script passages, mainly into Elvish, and wrote Elvish lyrics for the soundtrack — [elvish.org Gwaith [snippet]](https://elvish.org/gwaith/movie_intro.htm)
- Tolkien left Sindarin with less vocabulary than Quenya, so Salo had to coin new words that fit attested patterns — [Confusing Middle 2025 [snippet]](https://confusingmiddle.com/2025/03/13/tolkiens-languages-in-film)
- Purists criticize such "Neo-Elvish" as bordering on fanon. Some argue the language in Salo's *A Gateway to Sindarin* (2004) "is not Sindarin but an arbitrary fabrication" — [Tolkien Gateway: Neo-Elvish [snippet]](https://tolkiengateway.net/wiki/Neo-Elvish)

**The Witcher's Elder Speech (Sapkowski, then Peterson for Netflix)**
- Sapkowski's books contain only fragments of Elder Speech, drawing on Welsh, Irish and Scottish Gaelic, German and English. For the Netflix series (2019), Peterson expanded these fragments into a full spoken language and changed the romanization — [Redanian Intelligence [snippet]](https://redanianintelligence.com/2019/12/17/game-of-thrones-david-j-peterson-explains-how-he-created-the-witchers-elder-speech); [Looper [snippet]](https://looper.com/818198/how-elder-speech-from-the-witcher-was-actually-created); [Screen Rant [snippet]](https://screenrant.com/witcher-netflix-elder-language-changes-explained/)
- Peterson published extensive Elder Speech material in 2020 — [Redanian Intelligence 2020 [snippet]](https://redanianintelligence.com/2020/07/19/the-witcher-language-creator-publishes-extensive-material-including-information-about-a-mysterious-new-character)

**Wenja (Ubisoft, *Far Cry Primal*, 2016)**
- Ubisoft found Andrew Byrd's recordings of reconstructed Proto-Indo-European and hired Andrew and Brenna Byrd (University of Kentucky) to lead a team of linguists — [Wikipedia: Wenja [snippet]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wenja_language); [Xbox Wire 2016 [snippet]](https://news.xbox.com/en-us/2016/02/24/resurrecting-a-dead-language-in-far-cry-primal/)
- The team estimated about 95% of the game's language was reconstructed from PIE, with about 5% "educated guesses." They built three tribal varieties (Wenja, Udam, Izila) and borrowed features from Hittite — [Wikipedia: Wenja [snippet]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wenja_language); [UKNow [snippet]](https://uknow.uky.edu/campus-news/uk-professors-go-primal-far-cry-0)
- Brenna Byrd trained the cast in Toronto through immersion, and the actors soon "started randomly shouting out things in Wenja" — [Wikipedia: Wenja [snippet]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wenja_language)

### Inferences
- Back-fitting is the industry norm, not the exception. Klingon started from a few TMP lines, Dothraki from about 50 book words, High Valyrian from two sentences and some names, Na'vi from about a dozen Cameron words, and Elder Speech from Sapkowski's fragments. In each case the creator treated the pre-existing words as fixed constraints and designed phonology and morphology that explain them. The *valar* conjugation case is the clearest example.
- Launch scope was "grammar plus enough lexicon for the script plus margin": about 1,700 words for Dothraki at pitch and about 500 for Na'vi at release. Lexicons then grew 3 to 6 times over a decade as sequels and fans created demand.
- The standard actor packet has four parts: English, conlang text, phonetic respelling, and audio at several speeds. A 4-page pronunciation guide anchored to English words is enough to standardize names and lines across many performers.
- Two drift sources recur. Performers and showrunners change pronunciation (*khaleesi*), and parallel content creators add words faster than the master database (Skyrim designers; TNG staff writers). Both argue for one machine-checkable source of truth.
- A gameplay-first language (Dovahzul) can be shallow at launch. If fans or later products will extend it, though, its lack of generative depth becomes a liability: Pagliarulo had to coin "love" after release.

### Gaps
- No primary source found for Okrand adapting grammar to actors' on-set mistakes. This story is widely repeated, but this research did not verify it. Okrand did say his own mistakes become part of the language (see Section 2).
- No reliable figure found for Dovahzul's total in-game word count.
- The research did not cover *Kingdom Come: Deliverance*, which uses real historical languages, not a conlang, as far as the researcher knows. It also did not cover Atlantean (Okrand), *Dune*'s Chakobsa (Peterson), or *Avatar: Frontiers of Pandora*.
- The research did not find what exactly Peterson's 2009 pitch document contained, beyond the 1,700-word and "complete grammar" figures. It also did not find Paul Frommer's actor-coaching workflow; the Rolling Stone article was paywalled.

## 2. Canon authority and extension: governance models for multi-contributor languages

### Takeaway
There are four working models:
1. **Single creator with a curated community record** (Klingon/KLI, Na'vi/LEP).
2. **Founding text that can never change, plus an academy that adds words** (Esperanto).
3. **Frozen baseline with only some word classes open** (Lojban).
4. **Descriptive canon set by usage surveys, kept as an open GitHub dataset** (Toki Pona/Linku).

All of them separate a protected core grammar from a growing lexicon. All of them require every word to carry a citation to its source. All of them handle conflicts through a defined decider: a person, an academy, a freeze rule, or survey thresholds.

### Cited Findings

**Klingon: one creator as sole authority, with the KLI as archivist**
- The KLI's canon rule: "only words from confirmed Okrand sources will be accepted, and the source must be cited" — [KLI New Klingon Words [fetched]](https://www.kli.org/about-klingon/new-klingon-words/)
- Each new-word entry carries a provenance tag for the venue where Okrand revealed it. Examples include "qep'a' 32" (the KLI's annual conference), "qepHom 2024" (the Saarbrücken meeting), the *HolQeD* journal, and books such as *Conversational Klingon*, *Power Klingon* and *The Klingon Way* — [KLI [fetched]](https://www.kli.org/about-klingon/new-klingon-words/)
- Tags seen on the KLI lists run up to "qep'a' 33," and some 2024 entries are tagged to *HolQeD* 2024. The T-page shows a last-updated date of April 14, 2025 — [KLI word lists [snippet]](https://www.kli.org/about-klingon/new-klingon-words/all)
- Curation passed from Will Martin (charghwI') to Alan Anderson (ghunchu'wI'), and now to a team led by Tali Smith (tIjey). "The choice of what words deserve to be included in this list is up to the team." The list "is subject to additions, deletions, and emendations as our knowledge increases" — [KLI [fetched]](https://www.kli.org/about-klingon/new-klingon-words/)
- Okrand's own canon principles:
  - "any Klingon spoken during TNG counts as legitimate Klingon, whether I made it up or not." He has incorporated all of it.
  - When he makes a mistake, "it becomes part of the language just because I said it."
  - "the language has taken on a life of its own."
  - Source: [StarTrek.com pt 2 [fetched]](https://www.startrek.com/news/qapla-klingon-language-creator-marc-okrand-part-2)

**Na'vi: one gatekeeper, a community pipeline for word proposals, and a blog as the publication of record**
- Learn Na'vi's Lexical Expansion Project (LEP) lets ordinary community members create Na'vi words and submit them anonymously for Frommer to consider. Proposals must follow Frommer's word-building and syllable rules — [Looper [snippet]](https://www.looper.com/1144324/avatar-superfans-have-expanded-the-navi-language-in-mindblowing-ways); [Kelutral wordbuilding [snippet]](https://kelutral.org/references/wordbuilding)
- Frommer: "I am still the only gatekeeper." He prefers to go slowly and "give each word its own thought" rather than generate words in bulk with AI — [Rolling Stone [snippet; paywalled]](https://www.rollingstone.com/tv-movies/tv-movie-features/avatar-the-way-of-water-real-life-navi-language-discord-james-cameron-depression-1234649601)
- The LEP plus Frommer have expanded the lexicon by more than 50% — [Wikipedia: Na'vi [snippet]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Na%CA%BCvi_language)
- Frommer's blog Na'viteri is still active (latest post October 31, 2026). Posts:
  - add vocabulary in batches ("Thirty-two new words"), noting that most new words "stem from the backlog of suggestions conveyed to me by members of the LEP";
  - settle grammar disputes after community comment. For example, in April 2026: "When interrogative <pe> is a suffix, it precedes case endings";
  - publicly reverse earlier positions ("kaltxì can be a noun… we'll add that to the dictionary listing").
  - Source: [naviteri.org [fetched]](https://naviteri.org/)

**Esperanto: a founding text no one may change, plus an academy that adds words**
- The Declaration of Boulogne (first World Esperanto Congress, August 9, 1905), Article 4, makes the *Fundamento de Esperanto* "the only basis of the Esperanto language binding on all Esperantists, which no one has the right to change" — [Wikipedia: Fundamento [fetched]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamento_de_Esperanto)
- Zamenhof's foreword expected that once new words stabilized, "some authoritative institution shall put them into an official dictionary." That institution is the Akademio de Esperanto, which issues the *Oficialaj Aldonoj* (Official Additions). These have authority equal to the Fundamento — [Wikipedia: Fundamento [fetched]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamento_de_Esperanto)
- Sources disagree on the number of Official Additions to date (ten vs. nine) — [Wikipedia: Fundamento](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamento_de_Esperanto) vs. [other summaries [snippet]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akademio_de_Esperanto)
- The Akademio took its current form in 1948, when the Lingva Komitato and the Akademio merged — [Wikipedia: Akademio [snippet]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akademio_de_Esperanto)

**Lojban: a frozen baseline with some word classes left open**
- LLG's Official Baseline Statement:
  - Scope: "the language design (baseline) will be frozen, excepting only for the addition of new lujvo, fu'ivla, and cmene." These are compounds, borrowings and names. Experimental cmavo in a reserved experimental space are also exempt.
  - Length: the freeze lasts "a minimum 5 years after its initial declaration" and "may be extended on a year by year basis."
  - During the freeze: "LLG will not consider any change proposals to the language." Proposals must be written up in Lojban and are only collected, not formally considered.
  - After the freeze: "further language evolution will NOT take place by prescriptive changes."
  - Rationale: "the desire of the community for language stability" and "a single image of what the standard for the language is." The commission (byfy/BPFK) was told to favor the status quo when unsure. It could fix typos and inconsistencies only by consensus and with documentation.
  - Timeline: the target was to declare the baseline complete in 2003.
  - Source: [LLG Official Baseline Statement [fetched]](https://tiki.lojban.org/Official+Baseline+Statement)
- In 2010, BPFK chair Robin wrote an essay arguing that the 2002 policy had caused serious harm, and proposed moving authority wholly to the BPFK — [Lojban list [snippet]](https://groups.google.com/g/lojban/c/M9Jug4X-0pc)

**Toki Pona: descriptive canon set by usage surveys, kept as an open GitHub dataset**
- Linku runs an annual community survey during *suno pi toki pona* (the language's anniversary). The survey assigns each word a usage category. These categories replaced the older "book presence" categories (pu/ku) and are "more granular, more frequently updated" — [linku.la/about [fetched]](https://linku.la/about); [sona.pona.la: Usage category [snippet]](https://sona.pona.la/wiki/Usage_category)
- Category thresholds, by share of respondents who use the word:

  | Category | Share of respondents |
  |---|---|
  | Core | 90–100% |
  | Widespread | 70–90% |
  | Common | 50–70% |
  | Uncommon | 20–50% |
  | Rare | 10–20% |
  | Obscure | 2–10% |
  | Sandbox | below the inclusion cutoff |

  The sandbox cutoff was 2% and was raised to 5% by consensus after a few days. Sources conflict on which applies — [sona.pona.la usage surveys [snippet]](https://sona.pona.la/wiki/usage_surveys)
- 2023 results: 118 core words, then 10 widespread, 7 common, 14 uncommon, 11 rare and 66 obscure, for 226 words through "obscure." The 2022 survey had 345 respondents — [sona.pona.la [snippet]](https://sona.pona.la/wiki/usage_surveys)
- Sonja Lang's own *Toki Pona Dictionary* (lipu ku, 2021) is itself survey-based and descriptive. It lists core words plus less-used words reported by fewer respondents — [Wikisource: Toki Pona Dictionary [snippet]](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Toki_Pona_Dictionary)
- The Linku dataset ("sona") is on GitHub, with one TOML file per word in `words/`. English source text and translations sync with Crowdin. A validation pipeline generates schemas from zod definitions, type-checks the TOML, validates cross-references and packages JSON. To contribute, "fork the repo, edit… and submit a pull request." Discussion happens in the kulupu Linku Discord. The repo owners are kala Asi and jan Kekan San — [github.com/lipu-linku/sona [fetched]](https://github.com/lipu-linku/sona); [linku.la/about [fetched]](https://linku.la/about)

### Inferences
- The models that work share one structure:
  - a **closed core**: phonology, morphology and function words, which change rarely and only by a high bar;
  - an **open periphery**: content words, compounds and names, which grow through a lightweight process.
  
  Lojban's freeze exemption for compounds, borrowings and names, and Esperanto's Fundamento plus Additions, are explicit versions of this. Klingon and Na'vi do the same implicitly, since grammar rarely changes and words are added in batches.
- **Provenance tags are the backbone of canon.** KLI's "qep'a' 32" and "qepHom 2024" tags are exactly what a YAML fact record should carry: a source, a ratifying event or version, and a date.
- **Single-gatekeeper models scale only while the gatekeeper is alive and engaged.** Okrand and Frommer are aging creators. Neither the KLI nor the LEP has a published succession rule. Esperanto (an institution) and Linku (a survey-and-dataset process) survive without their founders.
- **Usage-survey canon (Linku) suits a decentralized community well.** It replaces "who has authority?" with "what do speakers actually use?", and it has a built-in sandbox tier for not-yet-canonical words. The cost: survey canon lags behind and can drift from design intent. It also needs a large enough active speaker base (about 345 respondents).
- **Freezes buy stability at the cost of momentum.** The Lojban critique suggests a freeze needs a scheduled, rule-bound unfreeze path. "Proposals must be written in Lojban" is a creative gate that filters proposals toward fluent users.
- **Anonymous submission (LEP) reduces social and political pressure on the gatekeeper.** This is relevant where contributors hold voting power.

### Gaps
- No published formal procedure was found for how the KLI handles conflicts between Okrand's statements and on-screen Klingon, beyond his "it counts" principle.
- No published LEP rules document was found (vote thresholds, rejection rates, how long the queue takes).
- The current status (2026) of Lojban's baseline freeze, and the CLL 1.1 / BPFK work since 2010, was not established.
- The exact dates and contents of Esperanto's Official Additions were not retrieved from the Akademio's own site (akademio-de-esperanto.org).

## 3. Legal and licensing: is a language copyrightable, and what licensing choices exist?

### Takeaway
No U.S. court has ruled that a constructed language is or is not copyrightable. The Axanar court explicitly declined to decide the question. The prevailing expert view (the LCS, Peterson, Samuelson's §102(b) scholarship, and legal commentary) is this: dictionaries, grammars and specific dialogue are protected expression, while the language as a usable system is not. In practice, franchises control "officialness" through copyright in specific works, through trademarks, and through published fan-use guidelines, not through ownership of the language. Community conlang datasets use Creative Commons licenses (Linku uses CC BY-SA 4.0). Some founders renounced their rights outright (Zamenhof).

### Cited Findings
- **The LCS amicus brief in *Paramount v. Axanar*, filed April 27, 2016:**
  - Its arguments: works *about* a conlang (dictionaries) and works *in* a conlang (stories, poetry) get full copyright protection. However, "a constructed language *itself* is not protected, and should not be. Copyright law is simply too blunt a tool for this." Protecting a language would create a monopoly beyond what is needed to protect original works, and would chill use. The LCS also commissioned a Dentons legal memo on conlangs under U.S. IP law.
  - How the court handled it: on May 10, 2016, the court denied leave to file, without prejudice, stating it "does not reach the issue of whether languages, and specifically the Klingon language, are copyrightable." The January 2017 summary-judgment order said only that Klingon "may not be individually original and copyright protectable." Axanar's motion in limine 4 was tentatively granted, which kept Paramount from telling the jury that it owns Klingon.
  - Source: [LCS Axanar page [fetched]](https://conlang.org/axanar/)
- The LCS's renewed brief was denied as moot in light of the summary-judgment motions. No final ruling on Klingon's copyrightability exists. The LCS's claim of "victory" is its own characterization, not a holding — [LCS](https://conlang.org/axanar/); [FindLaw/Reason coverage [snippet]](https://reason.com/2016/04/28/paramount-copyright-claim-on-klingon-lan/)
- **Theory:** Pamela Samuelson argues that Congress meant 17 U.S.C. §102(b) to codify *Baker v. Selden*'s exclusion of "procedures, processes, systems, and methods of operation." This is the main doctrinal basis for treating a language as an unprotectable system — [Samuelson, Berkeley Law [snippet]](https://www.law.berkeley.edu/center-article/why-copyright-law-excludes-systems-and-processes-from-the-scope-of-its-protection/)
- Osgoode's IPilogue argues that CBS and Paramount may own copyright in specific Klingon dialogue and in Okrand's books, but cannot claim the language as a whole. One commentator took the opposite view: because Klingon was specially created for Paramount as part of Star Trek, Paramount has a "strong case" — [IPilogue [snippet]](https://www.yorku.ca/osgoode/iposgoode/?p=29238); [IP Intelligence Report [snippet]](https://www.ipintelligencereport.com/blogs/is-paramount-going-where-no-copyright-holder-has-gone-before)
- **Peterson's view:**
  - Okrand's documents and dictionary "absolutely can be copyrighted," but "you can copyright the book but not the words in it or how people use them."
  - He compares a language to a recipe: the book is protected, the ingredients and the dish are not.
  - "The minute it's created, anyone can use it."
  - Source: [Salon, May 2016 [fetched]](https://www.salon.com/2016/05/04/does_klingon_belong_to_everyone_its_a_language_the_whole_point_is_to_use_it)
- **Community norm that respects the copyright in the dictionary:** the KLI's new-words list deliberately omits vocabulary from *The Klingon Dictionary* and *Klingon for the Galactic Traveler*, "in order not to compete with Dr. Okrand's income via royalties" — [KLI [fetched]](https://www.kli.org/about-klingon/new-klingon-words/)
- **How the franchise actually controls fan work:**
  - In June 2016, CBS and Paramount issued ten "fan film guidelines." They promise not to sue non-professional, amateur productions that comply, and they cap length and commercial activity — [Techdirt [snippet]](https://archive.techdirt.com/articles/20160623/18105034806/as-cbs-paramount-continue-lawsuit-over-fan-film-it-releases-ridiculous-impossible-fan-film-guidelines.shtml)
  - The settlement on January 20, 2017 required Axanar to acknowledge that its works "were not approved by Paramount or CBS" and to follow the guidelines in the future. The settlement came after a ruling that *Prelude to Axanar* was not fair use — [Space.com [snippet]](https://www.space.com/35436-star-trek-axanar-fan-film-lawsuit-settled.html); [FWRV [snippet]](https://www.fwrv.com/news-articles/star-trek-copyright-suit-settled-fan-filmmakers-get-guidance/)
  - A September 2023 arbitration found Peters had breached the settlement and awarded CBS and Paramount $292,372.54 in costs — [Plagiarism Today [snippet]](https://www.plagiarismtoday.com/2023/10/23/axanar-creator-faces-new-legal-threat/amp/)
  - "STAR TREK and related marks are trademarks of CBS Studios Inc." — [StarTrek.com footer [fetched]](https://www.startrek.com/news/qapla-klingon-language-creator-marc-okrand-part-2)
- **Licensing precedents:**
  - The Linku/sona dataset is licensed CC-BY-SA-4.0, and the Linku website is GPL-3 — [GitHub sona [fetched]](https://github.com/lipu-linku/sona); [linku.la/about [fetched]](https://linku.la/about)
  - Sonja Lang's *Toki Pona Dictionary* (2021) is "copyright Sonja Lang." She later released pages 5 to 13 ("Notes on lipu pu") under CC BY 4.0 in January 2022 — [Wikisource [snippet]](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Toki_Pona_Dictionary)
  - Zamenhof renounced all personal rights to Esperanto in the *Unua Libro* front matter, which put it in the public domain. Sources differ on whether this first appeared in 1887 or 1888 — [Wikipedia: Unua Libro [snippet]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unua_Libro); [Benjamins chapter [snippet]](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1075/wlp.10.c08/pdf)
- **Tolkien:** the Elvish Linguistic Fellowship's journals *Vinyar Tengwar* and *Parma Eldalamberon* edit and publish Tolkien's unpublished linguistic papers. Neo-Sindarin coinages "remain provisional until attested there" in the community's view — [Elfdict / Tolkien Gateway [snippet]](https://tolkiengateway.net/wiki/Neo-Elvish)

### Inferences
- **For DDU, "canon" must be a governance status, not a property right.** If copyright cannot reliably stop anyone from using Norsund, then "official Norsund" can only mean "matches the ratified lexicon and grammar at version X." That status can be signalled by a certification mark or "Canon-compliant" badge, a trademark on the DDU or Norsund name, and a public validator. It cannot be enforced through the language itself.
- **The Paramount model** combines owning the reference books, owning the trademarks, and publishing fan guidelines. A decentralized franchise can copy the guidelines part (what derived products may claim) without a single corporate owner.
- **Licensing the lexicon:**
  - CC0 or CC BY maximizes adoption by commercial derived products such as games and merch, and avoids licensing friction. This matches the "language is free" norm.
  - CC BY-SA (Linku) keeps derivative dictionaries open, but can deter commercial game studios that do not want share-alike obligations on bundled data.
  - A common split: put the lexicon and grammar data under CC0 or CC BY, and keep narrative lore and art under a separate license or trademark policy.
- **Who owns what contributors submit:** "Work-for-hire" ambiguity (who owns what a contributor submits) is a real risk in a PR-based repo. A contributor license agreement, a DCO sign-off, or a repo-wide CC0 dedication on contribution avoids later disputes over individual coined words.

### Gaps
- No law-review article dedicated specifically to conlang copyrightability was retrieved, and the Dentons memo the LCS commissioned was not found online.
- No source found on the contract terms between HBO and Peterson, or Lightstorm/Disney and Frommer, for example whether the languages were work-for-hire.
- Lojban's licensing status was not confirmed (the researcher believes LLG placed the language in the public domain, but did not verify it). The current tokipona.org license for the official word list was not confirmed either.
- No non-U.S. legal analysis was found, for example on EU or UK copyright in languages or databases. Note that the EU *sui generis* database right could apply to a curated lexicon dataset. This needs checking.

## 4. Fan engagement and adoption: what drives people to learn and use a conlang

### Takeaway
Adoption follows on-screen exposure, but a language lasts only if there is a learnable reference and an ongoing stream of officially sanctioned new material. Examples of reference material: Duolingo courses, published dictionaries and Living Language books. Examples of sanctioned new material: qep'a' word releases, Na'viteri batches, Linku surveys. App courses voiced by the creator and timed to release dates produced the largest learner counts.

### Cited Findings
- **Duolingo High Valyrian:**
  - Peterson worked with Duolingo from October 2016 and wrote and voiced the course. It launched in July 2017, ahead of GoT season 7, with more than 2,000 words in the official vocabulary. It was web-only at first — [Quartz [snippet]](https://qz.com/1027693/game-of-thrones-duolingo-now-offers-a-high-valyrian-language-course); [GameSpot [snippet]](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/you-can-now-learn-high-valyrian-on-duolingo-and-use-it-to-raise-your-own-ar-dragon/1100-6505732/)
  - By 2019 it had about 740,000 "active learners" (people who completed at least one lesson). It was updated in April 2019 with new content and audio — [Marketplace [snippet]](https://www.marketplace.org/2019/03/13/how-one-linguist-teaching-game-thrones-fans-how-speak-high-valyrian)
  - A *House of the Dragon* update (2022) added 159 new words and more than 700 new sentences. Duolingo's blog cites 514,000 active learners (the date of that count is unclear) — [Duolingo blog [snippet]](https://blog.duolingo.com/high-valyrian-course-updates)
  - A CBS headline cited 1.2M people learning it — [CBS [snippet]](https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/baltimore/news/the-game-of-thrones-language-that-1-2m-people-are-learning)
- **Duolingo Klingon:** launched in March 2018 after more than 170,000 people expressed interest. Lead course creator: Felix Malmenbeck. It was still described as beta in 2022 — [TribLive [snippet]](https://archive.triblive.com/business/technology/duolingo-launches-klingon-language-learning-course); [Inquirer 2022 [snippet]](https://lifestyle.inquirer.net/?p=289311)
- Peterson codified Dothraki in *Living Language Dothraki* (2014) — [Wikipedia: Dothraki [snippet]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dothraki_language)
- **Na'vi:** the community (Learn Na'vi, Kelutral, Discord) is still active 17+ years after the film, sustained by Frommer's regular word batches and community writing contests on Na'viteri — [naviteri.org [fetched]](https://naviteri.org/); [Rolling Stone [snippet]](https://www.rollingstone.com/tv-movies/tv-movie-features/avatar-the-way-of-water-real-life-navi-language-discord-james-cameron-depression-1234649601)
- **Klingon:** a recurring calendar sustains the community: an annual KLI qep'a', the German qepHom, the *HolQeD* journal, and Okrand appearing at these events to reveal words — [KLI [fetched]](https://www.kli.org/about-klingon/new-klingon-words/)
- **Toki Pona:** Linku turns the annual survey into a community ritual on the language's anniversary. Its data powers several downstream tools (linku.la, nimi.li, a wiki and a Discord bot) — [linku.la/about [fetched]](https://linku.la/about); [GitHub sona [fetched]](https://github.com/lipu-linku/sona)
- **Skyrim:** a fan's request for a word for "love" led the original designer to coin one, *Ros*, years after release — [Bethesda [fetched]](https://elderscrolls.bethesda.net/en-AU/news/how-bethesda-game-studios-made-skyrims-dragon-language)

### Inferences
- **Four adoption drivers:**
  1. Exposure: screen or game time with memorable, repeatable phrases ("Valar morghulis," "Fus Ro Dah," "Qapla'").
  2. A free, structured on-ramp, such as an app course or a dataset with a UI.
  3. Ritualized releases of new official material.
  4. A sense that one's contributions can become canon (the LEP, Linku surveys, coinings made at fans' request).
- **Machine-readable data multiplies the tools built on it.** For DDU, a YAML lexicon with a stable schema can feed games, Discord bots, flashcards and font tooling at the same time, as Linku's JSON build does.

### Gaps
- No current (2025–2026) confirmation was found of whether Duolingo's High Valyrian and Klingon courses are still offered or updated. Learner figures conflict and are dated.
- No hard data found on Na'vi, Toki Pona or Dovahzul speaker counts.

## 5. Practical guidance for writers, voice actors and derived products using a conlang

### Takeaway
Writers should use a conlang sparingly and consistently. Lines go through the language steward. Actors get English, conlang text, a phonetic respelling, and audio at several speeds. A short pronunciation guide anchored to English, with explicit "never like X" warnings, standardizes names across performers. The romanization should signal unusual sounds. Writers should also expect and record on-set deviations, not fight them.

### Cited Findings
- **Request-and-deliver workflow:** producers mark the lines, and the conlanger returns text plus audio (normal speed, slow, syllable by syllable) — [CBS [snippet]](https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/baltimore/news/the-game-of-thrones-language-that-1-2m-people-are-learning); [TED Ideas [snippet]](https://ideas.ted.com/?p=111825)
- Okrand delivered transcripts and MP3s to the actors and the dialogue coach, from script excerpts only — [StarTrek.com pt 2 [fetched]](https://www.startrek.com/news/qapla-klingon-language-creator-marc-okrand-part-2)
- **Pronunciation guide pattern:** one entry per letter, each with an English or European anchor word, a "never like…" warning, physical cues (hand test, tongue position), and one compact stress rule. It also flags which sounds may legitimately vary by speaker, which turns accent variation into lore rather than error — [Peterson HV Pronunciation Guide [primary PDF]](https://dedalvs.com/work/house-of-the-dragon/misc/pronunciation_guide.pdf)
- **Romanization as an acting aid:** Klingon's capitals mark non-English sounds — [CNN [snippet]](https://amp.cnn.com/cnn/style/article/star-trek-klingon-marc-okrand). Peterson changed Elder Speech romanization for the Netflix series — [Screen Rant [snippet]](https://screenrant.com/witcher-netflix-elder-language-changes-explained/)
- **Deviations are absorbed:** showrunners chose a different *khaleesi* pronunciation, actors alter lines, and editors cut sentences — [Film Companion [snippet]](https://filmcompanion.in/features/hollywood-features/game-of-thrones-linguist-david-peterson-on-creating-the-dothraki-language); [TED Ideas [snippet]](https://ideas.ted.com/?p=111825). Okrand treated TNG staff-written Klingon and his own errors as canon — [StarTrek.com pt 2 [fetched]](https://www.startrek.com/news/qapla-klingon-language-creator-marc-okrand-part-2)
- **Scope to function:** Dovahzul's three-word shouts and the need for lyrics that rhyme in two languages shaped the phonology and rhythm. The language only had to be deep enough for gameplay — [Bethesda [fetched]](https://elderscrolls.bethesda.net/en-AU/news/how-bethesda-game-studios-made-skyrims-dragon-language)
- **Immersion training for a game cast:** Wenja's cast was immersed in the language and began improvising in it — [Wikipedia: Wenja [snippet]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wenja_language)
- **Keeping one master database:** Skyrim's two-way database fell behind when many designers coined words in parallel — [Bethesda [fetched]](https://elderscrolls.bethesda.net/en-AU/news/how-bethesda-game-studios-made-skyrims-dragon-language)

### Inferences
- **Recommended Norsund "writer kit":**
  1. A one-page pronunciation guide in the HV style.
  2. A name-formation guide for people, places, clans and ships, with patterns and banned clusters.
  3. A list of 50 to 100 high-frequency "flavor" words and set phrases that any contributor may use without review: greetings, oaths, curses, toasts, star and sky terms.
  4. A rule that any full sentence of Norsund in a canon product must be checked by the language process and logged with a source tag.
  5. Reference audio for names and set phrases.
- **Subtitling heuristic (the researcher's inference, not sourced):**
  - Subtitle when the plot depends on the content.
  - Leave lines unsubtitled when the point is texture or alienness.
  - Use set phrases that recur often enough for audiences to learn them, as the GoT catchphrases did.
- **Record on-screen deviations as variants** (dialect, register or error) in the lexicon, rather than retconning them away. This mirrors Okrand's "it counts" stance.

### Gaps
- No primary-source statement was found from Peterson, Okrand or Frommer on subtitling policy (when to subtitle versus leave a line opaque). The researcher believes Peterson discusses this in *The Art of Language Invention* (2015) and on his blog, but did not retrieve it.
- No studio "naming bible" or consistency guide for a conlang was found publicly.

## 6. Lessons for the Drunken Dragon Universe (Norsund in a YAML/GitHub, vote-governed canon)

### Takeaway
DDU should combine two models:
1. **Esperanto/Lojban-style:** a small, versioned, hard-to-change core ("Norsund Fundamento": phonology, phonotactics, romanization, core morphology, about 100 to 200 function and core words).
2. **Klingon/Na'vi/Linku-style:** an open lexicon pipeline. Every word is a YAML record with provenance, passes automated phonotactic checks (as LEP proposals must follow Frommer's rules, and as Linku runs CI validation), sits in a "sandbox" tier until ratified by vote, and can later be re-tiered by usage data.

The language should be licensed openly, for example CC0 or CC BY. "Official" status for derived products should come from compliance with a versioned canon plus a DDU trademark or badge policy, not from ownership of the language.

### Cited Findings
- **Stable core plus regulated growth works across a century:** the Fundamento may not be changed, and additions carry equal authority once ratified by an institution — [Wikipedia: Fundamento [fetched]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamento_de_Esperanto)
- **Freeze grammar, keep compounds, borrowings and names open:** this was Lojban's explicit exception list — [LLG Baseline [fetched]](https://tiki.lojban.org/Official+Baseline+Statement)
- **Provenance-tagged canon list curated by a team, with a strict source rule** — [KLI [fetched]](https://www.kli.org/about-klingon/new-klingon-words/)
- **Community proposals, filtered by rules, approved by a gatekeeper, published in batches** — [naviteri.org [fetched]](https://naviteri.org/); [Kelutral [snippet]](https://kelutral.org/references/wordbuilding)
- **One file per word on GitHub, PR-based, schema-validated, CC BY-SA, with usage tiers including a sandbox** — [GitHub sona [fetched]](https://github.com/lipu-linku/sona); [sona.pona.la [snippet]](https://sona.pona.la/wiki/usage_surveys)
- **Back-fitting from scattered names is standard practice** (Dothraki, about 50 words; High Valyrian, two sentences plus names) — [Wikipedia: Valyrian [snippet]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valyrian_languages)
- **The language itself is probably not ownable; the reference works and dialogue are** — [LCS [fetched]](https://conlang.org/axanar/); [Salon/Peterson [fetched]](https://www.salon.com/2016/05/04/does_klingon_belong_to_everyone_its_a_language_the_whole_point_is_to_use_it)
- **Uncoordinated contributors outpace a manual database** — [Bethesda [fetched]](https://elderscrolls.bethesda.net/en-AU/news/how-bethesda-game-studios-made-skyrims-dragon-language)

### Inferences (design recommendations)
1. **Back-fit first.** Harvest every invented name and term already in the DDU YAML fact records into a "pre-Norsund attested corpus." Treat those forms as fixed constraints, as Peterson did with *valar morghulis*. Derive the phonotactics and morphology that explain most of them, and mark the outliers as loans, archaisms or dialect forms rather than renaming canon entities.
2. **Version the core like software.** Number the Fundamento (v1.0.0). Grammar or phonology changes require a major version and a supermajority, plus possibly a time lock (Lojban-style minimum freeze). Lexical additions are minor versions. Corrections are patches. Derived products declare "Norsund v1.x compliant."
3. **Give each word a YAML record with provenance.** Suggested fields:
   - `lemma`, `ipa`, `respelling`, `pos`, `gloss`;
   - `derivation` or `etymology` (root plus affixes);
   - `status`: sandbox, proposed, canon, deprecated or variant;
   - `tier`: an optional Linku-style usage tier;
   - `source`: the PR, the first appearance in a product, and the ratifying vote ID or version (like KLI's "qep'a' 32");
   - `added_in_version`, `contributors`, `notes`.
4. **Automate the steward's rules.** CI checks phonotactics, stress placement, homophone collisions, forbidden clusters, near-duplicate glosses and schema validity before a vote, as Linku validates TOML and LEP proposals must follow Frommer's syllable rules. Automation shrinks the subjective part of voting to meaning and taste.
5. **Use separate authority roles.** Voters decide whether a word is canon. A small language-steward role (an elected council or rotating stewards) holds a narrow, published veto for grammatical or phonological non-compliance only. This copies the Na'vi gatekeeper's quality function without making one person a single point of failure, a risk visible with Okrand and Frommer.
6. **Use a sandbox tier.** Derived products may use sandbox words, but must label them as non-canon. Later usage data, such as a Linku-style periodic survey or a count of how often products use the word, can promote a word or let it lapse.
7. **Batch releases as community rituals.** Hold periodic "Norsund lexicon releases" with changelogs, like Na'viteri batches or qep'a' reveals. They drive engagement and give derived products clean version targets.
8. **Canonize deviations instead of fighting them.** If a shipped game or story mispronounces or misuses a word, record it as a variant (dialect, register or in-world error) linked to its source, following Okrand's "it counts" principle. Do this only if it passes the core-grammar checks; otherwise mark it non-canon.
9. **License openly; control "official" through governance.** Dedicate the lexicon and grammar data to CC0 (or CC BY) so any game or artist can embed it. Require contributors to agree via DCO or CLA that their coinages enter under that license. Reserve the DDU or Norsund marks and an "Official Norsund" badge for products that pass the validator against a declared canon version. Optionally publish fan and derived-product guidelines, like Paramount's, but permissive.
10. **Ship a writer and actor kit with v1.0.** Include a pronunciation guide (in Peterson's HV format), a naming guide, a list of free-use set phrases, reference audio, and a request path for full sentences. Without it, many parallel contributors will improvise inconsistently, as TNG staff and Skyrim designers did.
11. **Scope to function, but stay generative.** Norsund needs enough real morphology that new contributors can coin words consistently. The Dovahzul "smoke and mirrors" approach fails once a community wants to extend the language.

### Gaps
- No case was found of a conlang governed by token- or stake-based voting. DAO-style governance of a language appears untested. The closest analogs are survey-based (Linku) and academy-based (Esperanto).
- The research found no evidence on how vote-based canon handles bad-faith or brigaded word proposals in a language context. General open-source governance research would be needed.
- How the EU database right or contributor moral rights would affect a CC0 lexicon dataset has not been verified.
